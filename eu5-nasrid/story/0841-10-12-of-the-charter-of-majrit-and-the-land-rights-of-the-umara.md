[Majrīṭ](place:majrit) had been a frontier fort when the amīr Muḥammad I built it on the Manzanares, to guard the road to [Ṭulayṭula](place:tulaytula) against the Christians of the north. Nearly six hundred years later it had become something else. The road it guarded now ran through the middle of the empire, and the town had grown along it faster than any other in the interior, a market of wool, grain and leather where the drovers of the meseta met the merchants of Ṭulayṭula. In the spring of 841 its burghers came to [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya) to ask the Emperor for a city charter.

The Emperor [Muḥammad](person:muhammad-ibn-ismail-ibn-saad) did not want to give it. "Why should I grant such a favour so far from the sea?" he asked the dīwān. "Won't the city merely wither and die?" Every dirham the crown spent, in his view, should float.

The two princes saw a different chance. [Ismāʿīl](person:ismail-ibn-muhammad-ibn-ismail) and [Aḥmad](person:ahmad-ibn-muhammad-ibn-ismail) had taken up the work their grandfather [Ismāʿīl](person:ismail-ibn-saad) had begun and never finished: the breaking of the umarāʾ. The great houses had lost their mercenaries, their lordships and their equality with the crown. They had kept their land. In the integrated territories of the north they still held their estates by right, could open new mines and fields as they pleased, raise villas, and bind the peasants a little tighter to the soil every year. The northern umarāʾ wanted Majrīṭ's charter to fail as little as the burghers did, because their wool and grain went through its market, and the princes made them pay for it. In Shawwāl 841 the charter was granted, and the umarāʾ of the north agreed in return that their Land Rights in the integrated territories would be unravelled, slowly, one estate at a time.

Nobody at court pretended that this was a fair bargain. To soften it, the crown granted the great houses a yearly stipend for the patronage of the arts, so that every noble household might keep its poets, musicians and copyists. The clerks of the treasury, who paid it, called it by its real name, the fund that kept the umarāʾ from open mutiny. The Emperor signed both orders without much interest. The princes had spent the winter working out exactly what each one was worth.

*Have We not established for them a safe sanctuary, to which are brought the fruits of all things as provision from Us?*
