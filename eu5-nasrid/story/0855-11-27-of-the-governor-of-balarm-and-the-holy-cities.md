The dīwān had agreed in the year of the conquest that Sicily should have a ṭāʾifa of its own when the Mamlūks' war was over. The Mamlūks' war was not over. Over the year 855 a second idea came to the court, and it grew stronger with every month the first was put off.

[Ismāʿīl](person:ismail-ibn-muhammad-ibn-ismail) argued for the ṭāʾifa. That was how the realm had always held what it took: under a house of the blood, bound by marriage, with its own court and its own levies. It was how the north had been held for forty years. [Aḥmad](person:ahmad-ibn-muhammad-ibn-ismail) argued for the crown. A ṭāʾifa king would build for himself, he said. A governor would build for the realm, and Sicily needed roads, mills and harbours more than it needed another court. The [Emperor](person:muhammad-ibn-ismail-ibn-saad) settled it, as he settled most things, by what pleased him. He did not want to give away the palace of the Norman kings, the cathedral, the harbour of Balarm and the wheat of the island to a cousin. Sicily would have a governor, answerable to the dīwān, and the ṭāʾifa would wait. Everyone at court understood "for now" in his own way.

Through all that year the news from the east was bad. The Mamlūks' war on the Banū Lām had gone wrong in the Ḥijāz. By the end of 854 the men of the Banū Lām held Makka and Madīna, with Jeddah and Yanbuʿ, their ports on the Red Sea. The Holy Cities were in the hands of the Mamlūks' enemies. The Mamlūks had gone to war to make themselves masters of the Red Sea coast, and they had lost the cities whose protection was their first title to rule. In Jumādā al-Ākhira 855 their army caught a column of the Banū Lām at al-Ukhaydir and destroyed it, but the cities were not retaken. The Emperor had stood in the Ḥaram himself eighteen years before. He took the news harder than anyone at court. Al-Andalus was at war beside Cairo and had not sent one ship or one man. Ismāʿīl said that Cairo had not asked. The Emperor said that Cairo should not have had to.

Meanwhile the treasury, which had been full at the start of the year, was spent almost to nothing. Most of it went where Aḥmad's gold always went. There were new paper mills at [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya), [al-Mariyya](place:al-mariyya) and [Shantarīn](place:shantarin), and above all at [Shāṭiba](place:shatiba). Shāṭiba had made the best paper in the west since before the Christians took it, and it was making it again under the crown. There were jewellers at [Qurṭuba](place:qurtuba), [Jayyān](place:jayyan) and [Sharīsh](place:sharish), a library at [al-Ushbūna](place:al-ushbuna), a quarry at Balarm for the governor's builders, and paved roads where there had been tracks. On 22 Dhū al-Qaʿda a grandson was born to Aḥmad, the son of his son Muḥammad. They named him Aḥmad.

No new land was found that year. The explorer [ʿAbd al-Jalīl](person:abd-al-jalil-al-qubbasi) was somewhere beyond the Cape, and the ships of the Kongo trade came and went. But by the turn of the year the voyages had changed the way the realm thought. In the madrasas of Ishbīliya students argued over the maps the pilots brought home. The merchants priced the wares of lands no Andalusī had seen, and the jurists asked what the law owed to peoples it had never heard of. By 2 Dhū al-Qaʿda 855 the court was speaking of the world as larger than the books had said. It did not yet know how much larger.

*Have they not travelled through the land and have hearts by which to reason and ears by which to hear? For indeed, it is not eyes that are blinded, but blinded are the hearts which are within the breasts.*
