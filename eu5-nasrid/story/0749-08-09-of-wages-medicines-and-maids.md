By the beginning of Rabīʿ al-Thānī the pestilence was in [Washqa](place:washqa) in the north and [Antaqīra](place:antaqira) in the west, and in the mountain villages of the Alpujarra. In Balansiyya it was in [Shāṭiba](place:shatiba) and [Qandiya](place:qandiya). The realm counted seventeen thousand fewer souls than in the autumn before. That was only the beginning, though no one yet knew it.

The plague killed the poor first and fastest, for they lived closest together. Within a season the masters of the workshops and the owners of the fields were crying that there were no hands to hire. Those labourers who were left knew their worth and asked double. In [Muyaqar](place:muyaqar) and the towns around it the guilds agreed among themselves to hold wages where they had been, and the labourers came to the governor to complain of it. The sultan was asked to judge. He sided with the guilds. The labourers, he said, must be shown their place, for a realm at war cannot let its wages run wild. The labourers went back to work. They did not forget, and their grumbling spread across the realm further than the governor reported.

In Jumādā al-Ākhira the physicians of the houses of healing came to the sultan with a darker complaint. Half the medicines sold in the markets were spoiled, they said, or cut with chalk and flour by merchants who had seen that a frightened man will pay anything. Worse, such filth might itself carry the sickness from house to house. The sultan ordered them burned. Officers went through the markets, and across the market of [Ishbīliyya](place:ishbiliyya), to which the trade of the realm answers, nearly half the stock of medicines went onto the fires. The merchants wept and the crown paid them a little to quiet them. The physicians said it was the best order of the year.

In Shaʿbān the Nobility came to the sultan with their own complaint. No servant would enter the house of a sick nobleman for any wage, and the great houses were left with no one to carry the water or wash the dying. They asked for the right to draft servants from among the common people. The sultan needed the nobles, who led his levies, more than he needed the goodwill of the villages. He granted it. Men and women of the Commoners were taken from their fields to serve in houses where the plague already was.

On the ninth of Shaʿbān the dīwān's account stood thus. The sickness had touched eleven of the realm's towns and and the pestilence itself had killed nine thousand of its people. In all the known world it had carried off more than twenty million since it rose at Yāyiq. Some at court hoped the worst had passed.

They were wrong.
