Al-Ḥājj Yūsuf had marched to knock Portugal out of the war, and his son kept to the plan. In the summer of 765 the army of Gharnāṭa went deep into Portugal and laid siege to [al-Ushbūna](place:al-ushbuna) itself. But the Portuguese army was not there. It had gone east to join the host of Arghūn, and together they were burning their way through the lands of the ṭāʾifas. So the army left a screen before the walls and marched east, under a general named [Murād](person:murad). He was a quiet man who kept his own company, and he planned every attack as carefully as a merchant counts his coin. He chased the joined host halfway across Iberia.

He caught them on the fourth of Dhū al-Qaʿda at Zafra. It was Castilian land that the Marīnids had taken, so that Muslims of the far shore held the field on which Muslims of this one fought. Murād had nine thousand eight hundred men, the sultan's army with a contingent of Balansiyya. The enemy had six thousand seven hundred and fifty, Portuguese and Aragonese under one command. The Portuguese commander was a capable man, and the Christian line held through the morning. In the afternoon Murād sent the jinetes of Gharnāṭa around its flank, and then he sent the thagrī footmen in. The Christian line broke, the Aragonese first. Three thousand three hundred and fifty of the enemy were left on the field, and two thousand two hundred of ours. It was not cheap, but it was a victory. The joined host of the two crowns no longer existed. Only its pieces were left, running west toward the one city they still hoped to hold.

Murād followed them all the way back to al-Ushbūna. Under its walls, on the twelfth of Dhū al-Ḥijja, he destroyed what was left of the Portuguese: sixteen hundred men, every one of them killed or taken, and not one of ours lost. The next day it was the Aragonese remnant's turn, seventeen hundred and seventy men, and again not one of ours. Those were the days of tashrīq, when the pilgrims at Minā cast their stones at the pillars of the Devil. The army of Gharnāṭa cast its own before the gates of al-Ushbūna.

Portugal had nothing left in the field, and it sued for peace the same day. It gave up its whole south: the Algarve that the old geographers called al-Gharb, with [Shilb](place:shilb), [Ukshūnuba](place:ukshunuba) and Tavira, and the lower plains of the Alentejo with [Bāja](place:baja), Mértola and Serpa. That was thirteen towns and eighty-three thousand souls. Portugal would also pay reparations for ten years. The court did not mean to keep these Portuguese Christians under its own hand. When the war was won they would be given a prince of their own, as Balansiyya and Mursiyya had been, and become a third ṭāʾifa of al-Andalus.

Qashtāla and Arghūn fought on. But the sultan's treasury, which had held twenty-four dīnārs on the day his father died, now held five hundred.

*How many a small company has overcome a large company by permission of God. And God is with the patient.*
