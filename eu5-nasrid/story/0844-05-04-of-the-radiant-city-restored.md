Three miles west of [Qurṭuba](place:qurtuba), on the slope where the Sierra Morena comes down to the plain, the caliph ʿAbd al-Raḥmān III had built a city for himself and named it Madīnat al-Zahrāʾ, the Radiant City. It had palaces of marble and onyx, a hall whose roof was said to be made of gold and silver tiles, gardens watered from the mountains, a mosque, a mint and barracks for the caliph's guard. Ambassadors from Constantinople and the German emperor had been received there and told to wait in halls designed to make them feel small. It had stood for less than eighty years. In the fitna of the eleventh century, when the caliphate tore itself apart, the Berber soldiers sacked it, and for four hundred years its stones had been carried off to build other men's houses. By the time the house of Naṣr took Qurṭuba back, it was a field of broken columns that the shepherds of the Sierra used as a sheepfold.

The Emperor [Muḥammad](person:muhammad-ibn-ismail-ibn-saad) went to see it in Jumādā al-Ūlā 844. He had been reading the histories of the caliphate, as he now read everything, and he walked the ruins for a whole day with the old books in the hands of a secretary, matching the descriptions to the stones. When he came back to [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya) he told the dīwān that he would rebuild it. Some in the dīwān said that a ruin was a ruin and that the money would be better spent on ships. He answered that every king and pretender in Christendom who doubted the empire's right to the peninsula should be made to wait in its halls, as their ancestors had waited. He paid for the first year's work out of his own treasury.

The masons, glassmakers and goldsmiths came from every city of the empire, and the merchants of Ishbīliya sent fine cloth for the hangings at prices that the clerks thought suspiciously low. A year later the halls of the Radiant City were standing again on the slope above Qurṭuba, built over the old foundations and as close to the old descriptions as the Emperor and his secretaries could make them. The court began to receive ambassadors there. The crown's hold on the country around it tightened, and the town below grew prosperous on the custom of the court. Across the peninsula, the court noticed, people had begun to speak of the Emperor as the caliphs' heir, which was what the court had intended.

*Do you construct on every elevation a sign, amusing yourselves, and take for yourselves palaces that you might abide eternally?*
