When the war chest was empty and the mercenaries had gone home, our lord [Saʿd](person:saad-ibn-yusuf) turned to the lands he had won. He did not like what the dīwān's reckoners showed him. Three realms were living under one crown, and only one of them was rich.

The first was the old kingdom of the mountains and the coast, from [Gharnāṭa](place:gharnata) to Mālaqa: eighteen towns and a quarter of a million souls, nearly all of them Muslim, and nearly three-quarters of what the land could yield reaching the treasury. The second was the valley of the great river, won from Qashtāla in the wars of the last twenty years: thirty-one towns and four hundred thousand souls, one in five of them Muslim, held by the crown with half a grip and yielding three-fifths of what it might. The third was the north shore of the Strait, taken from Fās only months before. There were eleven towns and eighty-five thousand souls, more than half of them Muslims, because the Marīnids had converted them. They had been fought over again and again in twenty years of war. Their fields were poorer than when the wars began, Runda poorest of all. Of every hundred dīnārs the north shore could have paid, less than one came to Gharnāṭa.

Then there was [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya). The reckoners counted seventy-seven thousand seven hundred souls in it, more than in Gharnāṭa itself, so the greatest city of the realm was not the sultan's capital. Its weavers made cloth fine enough for kings, its smiths made arms, and its paper-makers and copyists kept a scriptorium. It was still a Christian city in its tongue and in most of its prayers. But in the last four years the Muslims within its walls had grown from twenty thousand to twenty-four thousand, and the crown's hold on it had risen from less than half to more than half. In the merchants' reckoning its market had become the first in all the West, ahead of Fās. The sultan said that a city like that was worth more than a province of wheat, and that he would rebuild the north around it.

He began with the dīwān. [Maryam bint Ismāʿīl](person:maryam-bint-ismail), who had spent four years binding the new lands to the crown, was set to the same work as [Duʿā](person:dua-marinid): making the conquered towns Andalusī again. Duʿā took Ishbīliya and Maryam took [Jayyān](place:jayyan), which already had its madrasa. The sultan told them he did not want the Castilians driven out, because a weaver who left took his loom with him. He wanted their children to speak Arabic.

In Shaʿbān 772 the ʿulamāʾ came to him about the moneylenders. In the towns of the valley, they said, Muslim merchant families had begun to lend at interest, as the Jews and the Genoese did, and the practice was spreading. The qāḍī recited the verse: *Allāh has permitted trade and forbidden usury.* The court waited. The sultan had sat through every war of his reign in debt to his own estates, and he still owed seven loans. He answered that the ʿulamāʾ were overreacting, and that he would sooner have Muslims lending in Ishbīliya than Genoese. The ʿulamāʾ went away displeased, and they did not forget it.

The treasury now gained a little over ten dīnārs a month, and the sultan meant to spend all of it in the north. There were Castilian Patriots in arms in the hills, a claim against Qashtāla to be drawn up, and an annexation of Mursiyya under way. None of it would come cheap. But the court noted that this was the first sultan of the house of Naṣr to talk about rebuilding the towns of the valley instead of defending the passes against them.

*Allāh does not change the condition of a people until they change what is in themselves.*
