In the winter of 774 the last of the seven loans of the Marīnid war was paid back to the estates, every dirham of it, with the interest the dīwān had promised. The sultan had refused to think of a new war, or even of the pilgrimage, while he still owed his own lords money. A man who stood at ʿArafa in debt, he said, stood there with another man's purse. Now the treasury took in half again what it spent, every estate paid more into it than before, and even the north shore, which had yielded almost nothing at the peace, had begun to pay. His mother [Sayyida](person:sayyida-of-fezzan) had taken a seat in the dīwān beside [Duʿāʾ](person:dua-marinid), and a marriage had bound the ṭāʾifa of [Mursiyya](place:mursiyya) to Fezzan, her homeland beyond the desert. Only then did the sultan begin to make ready for the Hajj.

The truce with Arghūn ran out in Dhū al-Ḥijja of the year 774, on the day of the feast, and the sultan let it run out in [Gharnāṭa](place:gharnata) rather than at Minā. He had put off his pilgrimage for it, and he did not mean to waste the delay. By Rabīʿ al-Thānī 775 the dīwān's second claim was written, this one against the crown of the Catalans, and our lord [Saʿd](person:saad-ibn-yusuf) rode north at the head of the armies of the realm. He wintered them at [Balansiyya](place:balansiyya), within sight of the Upper March. The old map of the caliphs called that country the Thaghr al-Aʿlā, and no army of the house of Naṣr had ever marched into it.

Arghūn stood alone. Its king, [Joan](person:joan-of-aragon), was twenty-four and wore two crowns, Arghūn's and Ṣiqilliya's, but the second brought him only three ships and Ṣiqilliya's subjects across the sea. The Genoese, who were the Sicilians' friends, were busy with a war against Bologna and Pisa. The Pope at Avignon wrote letters and sent nothing. The dīwān reckoned the Catalan host at a few hundred men under arms against the sultan's three thousand three hundred. So the sultan raised no levies. He said the regulars would do, and that the peasants of the valley had a harvest to bring in.

On 14 Jumādā al-Ākhira 775 the herald read the declaration in Balansiyya. The same week, word came from Alexandria that the sultan [Būlād](person:bolot-mamluk) had answered the call as an ally should. The Mamlūk galleys had put out with the Egyptian vassals behind them, and they were sailing for the shores of Ṣiqilliya, so that Joan's second crown would have to look to itself.

King Joan chose not to sit out a siege. He called out the levies of Catalonia: archers from the mountain towns, foot from the coast, and a few hundred horsemen, among them the mailed knights of the estates around [Barshalūna](place:barshaluna). In Shaʿbān he brought five thousand three hundred men to meet the sultan's three thousand three hundred on the coastal plain below the city walls.

The sultan commanded in person. He set his heavy foot in the centre to hold whatever came at them. He gave the right wing, and two-thirds of all the horse, to his brother [Abū al-Ḥasan ʿAlī](person:ali-ibn-yusuf), who was eighteen and had never led men in battle. The sultan kept the left under his own banner. The plan was the old one of the Andalusī jinetes: hold the centre, ride round the wings, and close the jaws.

The battle lasted three weeks of manoeuvre, sallies and skirmishes in the winter rain, and it ran on into Ramaḍān. The jurists ruled that men in the field were travellers and might break the fast, and the sultan broke it with them so that none would be ashamed to eat. The centre did what was asked of it. It took the weight of the Catalan foot and the archers' arrows and did not move, while the two wings fell on the Aragonese flanks and destroyed them. By the end only the Catalan centre was left on the field: levies, and the Catalan knights gathered around them. Then the centre pushed forward. Abū al-Ḥasan's right wavered under the knights' last charge, and for a moment the court's watchers on the hill thought the day might turn. Then the sultan brought the left round on the Catalans' other side, and they were caught between the two wings.

It ended on 6 Ramaḍān. Three thousand three hundred and seventy-five of King Joan's men lay dead or scattered before the walls of his capital, against one thousand two hundred and twenty-eight of ours. Barshalūna's gates were shut, and the Catalan army had left the field. The court poets compared the battle to a woven cloth, with the centre the warp, the horse the weft, and the sultan the weaver, and for once the courtiers did not think they had exaggerated. It was the first Muslim victory before Barshalūna since the days of the caliphs. It had cost the realm more than a third of the army that fought it.

*And already had Allāh given you victory at Badr while you were few. So fear Allāh, that you may be grateful.*
