[ʿAbd al-Jalīl](person:abd-al-jalil-al-qubbasi) was born at al-Qubbāṣa, in the old lands of Portugal, two years after Portugal had ceased to be a kingdom, and he grew up an Andalusī of the umarāʾ in a country that had only lately become Andalusī. He had no great name to inherit, so he made one: the house he founded took its name from his birthplace, and men called him al-Qubbāṣī. He was a soldier more than a courtier. The officers who served under him said he could take a fortified harbour in a morning and could not hold a conversation at dinner. He had one passion besides war, and that was to know what lay beyond the last headland on the chart.

On 28 Rajab 849 the Emperor [Muḥammad](person:muhammad-ibn-ismail-ibn-saad) made him one of the explorers of the empire and sent him south. The pilots of [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya) had followed the coast of Africa for fifty years, past the Green Cape, past the gold coast, past the mouths of the great rivers, and every year the coast had gone on turning south and east and south again, as though the land had no end. ʿAbd al-Jalīl followed it further than any of them. In Dhū al-Ḥijja 849, the month of the pilgrimage, his ships came to a great cape where the land fell away and the coast turned at last toward the east. Beyond it there was open water. The pilots said that Africa had come to an end, and that the sea of the Indies might lie on the other side.

The merchants were quicker than the court. While the explorer's report was still being copied, the burghers of Ishbīliya sent factors to study the kingdom of the Kongo, whose river and markets the ships had passed on the way south. Its copper, its cloth and its ivory were already being priced in the funduqs.

In the same season the crown let go of the island of Fernando Po. The Jamāʿa of 847 had asked for it with one voice, and the burghers had offered to share the cost, but few settlers could be found to go so far, and the ships had no need of it: they went south along the coast of Mālī, where the Mansa's harbours and the trading posts of Kaabu served them well enough. The settlement was withdrawn, and the Emperor told the court that the island would wait until the sea road needed it. The lords who had hoped for estates there were not pleased. The burghers, who had found the Kongo, were less troubled than they had expected to be.
