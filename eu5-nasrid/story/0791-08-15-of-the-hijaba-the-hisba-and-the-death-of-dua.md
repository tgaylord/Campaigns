The Emperor returned from al-Qāhira with his head full of what he had seen there: the Mamlūk court with its offices and ranks, and the books the scholars of al-Azhar had opened for him on the government of the old caliphs. He meant to build on them. The first fruit was the Ḥijāba. Under the Umayyads of [Qurṭuba](place:qurtuba) the ḥājib had been the chamberlain of the court. He presided over his own council, received petitioners and embassies, and drafted decrees in the caliph's name. Under al-Manṣūr he became the caliph's power in all but name. The Emperor revived the office. It made the throne stronger and gave it something of the dignity of the caliphs' own court. Like the ḥājibs of old, it would also stand between the throne and the great lords, and the nobles noticed.

The Ḥijāba brought a new seat in the dīwān, and the Emperor filled it with his heir. The crown prince [Ismāʿīl](person:ismail-ibn-saad) had been at sea as an admiral, carrying the crown's authority over the coasts. Nobody took him for a fighting sailor, and he did not pretend to be one; he governed the coasts by persuasion and left the fighting to his captains. His father called him home, and on the first day of Muḥarram 789 he took his seat beside his aunt [Maryam](person:maryam-bint-yusuf), his uncle [Ismāʿīl](person:ismail-ibn-yusuf) and his mother. He was set to the Emperor's great project: the seminaries, the scholars, and the shaping of what the realm should prize. He was twenty, married now to [Bernarda](person:bernarda-of-murcia) of Mursiyya, and the court said he could talk a bird down from a tree.

Other lessons from the east took root in the towns. Across the realm the crown began to build hammams, beginning at [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya): bathhouses for washing, company and ritual purity, a cornerstone of Andalusī town life that the Castilians had let fall into ruin. From beyond the Pyrenees came news the court did not pretend to regret. Qashtāla had joined a war in the north on the side of a little Ardennes county against the king of France, and lost two armies in the hills of the Rhône.

In Dhū al-Ḥijja 790, in the last days of the year, [Duʿāʾ](person:dua-marinid) the Marīnid died. She was fifty years old. She had sat in the dīwān for thirty-three years, under two husbands and two reigns. She had brought the court foreign learning and kept the realm through the War of the Upper March as regent, and she had seen her son made heir. The court gave out that she died of a short illness. It was very short. The physicians were not called until it was over, and the household was quiet for a long time afterwards. The Emperor ordered her buried with every honour, in the Rawḍa beside the kings of the house, and he did not speak of her again in the dīwān.

More than seven months later, in Shaʿbān 791, the bureaucracy gained a third office, as the empire turned slowly toward the sea and its trade. This was the Ḥisba, the old inspectorate of the Islamic city. Its muḥtasib walked the markets, checked the weights and the measures, put down fraud and enforced honest dealing. The ʿulamāʾ approved, since the muḥtasib was one of their own. The merchants did not, since he was not one of theirs. The Emperor gave the first muḥtasib of Ishbīliya one order in his own words: to begin with the crown's warehouses, and to hold the Emperor's weights to the same measure as any grocer's in the sūq. The merchants still grumbled, but none of them could say the scales were weighted for the crown. The empire's goods sold better for being honestly weighed, and its markets moved a little further from barter and a little nearer to coin.

*O reassured soul, return to your Lord, well-pleased and pleasing to Him.*
