When Angra was founded on Terceira in 823, the empire had three towns in the Ocean. Seven years later it had eleven, and the clerks of the Dīwān al-Inshāʾ had to keep a separate register for them, because the islands were too far apart to be written up with any province of the mainland.

In the Canaries the settlers went where the islanders already were. Tamaraceite on Gran Canaria became a town of the crown in Jumādā al-Ūlā 824, with eight and a half thousand Guanches, and Tedote on La Palma followed in Rajab 828 with five and a half thousand more. Like the people of Maxorata, they were left to their own customs and their own gods, and they paid their dues to the Emperor's governor and were otherwise left alone.

The Green Cape was empty, and there the empire had to bring its own people. Santo Antão, the northernmost of the islands, became the empire's first town in the southern sea in Ramaḍān 825, with seventeen hundred Andalusī settlers. Fogo, the island of the fire mountain, followed in Rabīʿ al-Thānī 828. Santiago, the largest of the islands, followed in Shawwāl 830, settled by Portuguese-speaking Muslims of al-Gharb and Burtuqāl. The dīwān had told the first fleet that the islands were to be a land of their own one day, and the captains now spoke of them as the halfway house to Mālī and the Gulf, where a ship bound south could take on water, salt and goats.

The Azores filled from the west. After Angra came Santa Cruz on Flores, the furthest island of all, in Ṣafar 826; Horta on Faial in Shaʿbān 828; and on 11 Ṣafar 831 Ponta Delgada on São Miguel, the largest of the islands, each with twelve or thirteen hundred settlers. Unlike the Christian farmers of Angra, nearly all of them were Andalusīs and Muslims, and the first building in each town was a mosque. The pilots said that from Flores the sun set over nothing at all, and that no one knew how far the nothing went.

The Ocean paid for itself in unexpected ways. In Shaʿbān 824 envoys came to [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya) from Ethiopia, the Christian kingdom beyond Egypt, wanting to buy naft, the fire weapons the armies of al-Andalus had used since the days of the sultans. The dīwān warned that a weapon once sold could one day be turned against the seller. The Emperor [Ismāʿīl](person:ismail-ibn-saad) asked how far Ethiopia was from Ishbīliya and how much it was offering. It was a very long way, and the offer was four hundred and nineteen pieces of gold, which the treasurers reckoned at twice the crown's yearly income. He said that it would never backfire, and took the gold.

*And He has cast into the earth firmly set mountains, lest it shift with you, and rivers and roads, that you may be guided.*
