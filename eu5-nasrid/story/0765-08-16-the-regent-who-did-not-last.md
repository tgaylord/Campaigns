On the twelfth of Rabīʿ al-Thānī in the year 765 our lord [Saʿd](person:saad-ibn-yusuf) turned sixteen and took the government of the realm into his own hands. The regency of [Naṣr al-Istijjī](person:nasr-al-istijji) ended that morning, and so, by evening, did Naṣr. The court gave out that he had died of a sickness. The huntsmen who had ridden out with him told a different story, of a fall in the hills and a horse that came home without its rider, and were told to stop telling it. The chronicler writes down both and judges neither. God knows best.

Within days the young sultan made his choice of a second wife. He had one already, [Aiman](person:aiman-mamluk), daughter of the Mamlūk sultan, promised to him when he was three and married to him before his majority. But a sultan of al-Andalus needs the Maghrib as well as Egypt, and there was a Marīnid princess in his own palace. So Saʿd married [Duʿāʾ](person:dua-marinid): twice widowed, ten years his elder, the most capable mind in the dīwān, and the widow of his father.

The ʿulamāʾ were outraged, and they were right to be. The Book is plain on it: *do not marry those women whom your fathers married.* The jurists of Gharnāṭa said so from the pulpit of the great mosque, and the jurists of Qurṭuba said it more loudly still. Men who had loved al-Ḥājj Yūsuf said that his son had married his stepmother before the earth on his grave had settled. In the souks they said worse: that it was Duʿāʾ, not the boy, who had arranged it, and that a regent's fall from his horse had cleared the way for her. By the middle of Shaʿbān the satisfaction of the ʿulamāʾ stood at little more than a third. In the middle of a war that was a danger, not just a scandal.

Meanwhile the war went badly in the east. On the twenty-sixth of Jumādā al-Ūlā the Portuguese, whom the dead sultan had marched west to find, appeared instead at Cañete and broke the host of Balansiyya. In Rajab the ṭāʾifas attacked the joined armies of Portugal and Arghūn at Castellón and were thrown back.

The young sultan proved his gifts first in the council, not on the field. He summoned the Jamāʿa and gave the ʿulamāʾ what they had wanted for twelve years. The tax on the dhimmīs, remitted since 753 to quiet the Castilians of the valley, would be collected again. A new campaign of conversion would be preached in Qurṭuba and [Ishbīliyya](place:ishbiliyya) and every town the crown held. The jurists took the bargain. They did not forgive the marriage, but they stopped preaching against it. The Christians of the valley would pay for the sultan's peace with his clergy, as subjects so often pay for their rulers' sins. The old men of the court noted that a prince of sixteen had learned in his first spring how the ʿulamāʾ could be bought, and with whose money.

*And do not marry those women whom your fathers married, except what has already occurred. Indeed, it was an immorality and hateful, and evil as a way.*
