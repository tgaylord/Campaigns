*And proclaim to the people the Ḥajj; they will come to you on foot and on every lean camel, coming from every distant pass.*

No reigning sultan of the house of Naṣr, the court said, had ever made the pilgrimage. The road from Gharnāṭa to Makka is the longest in the Muslim world: across the whole sea, the whole of Egypt and the whole of the Ḥijāz. A prince of al-Andalus who leaves his throne for a year leaves it within a day's ride of Qashtāla. But the war was won and the pestilence was passing, and our lord [Yūsuf](person:yusuf-i) resolved to go. On the fifteenth of Jumādā al-Ākhira in the year 752, half a year before the season of the pilgrimage, he took ship for the East. His elder brother [Faraj ibn Ismāʿīl](person:faraj-ibn-ismail) governed the realm as regent. The caravan was stocked for a year and guarded like an army, and at its head went the *maḥmal* of Gharnāṭa, the empty litter that is the banner of a prince's pilgrimage. It was hung with brocade and carried in all the splendour the treasury could bear.

On the fifteenth of Shaʿbān, the night of the middle of the month, when the faithful say the record of the year is written, the caravan came through the northern gate of al-Qāhira. The city swallowed it at once: the noise, the spice, the tanneries and the roasting meat. Over the roofs rose al-Azhar, whose scholars are counted among the finest in Islam. The sultan might have sat with them, or pushed on at once for Makka. He did neither. He had the caravan dressed in banners and silk and kept it ten days in the city of the Mamlūks, so that the court whose daughter was promised to his son might see what the house of Naṣr could still put on the road.

On the thirteenth of Dhū al-Qaʿda the caravan came down into Makka, and the Grand Mosque opened before it. The Kaʿba stood at its heart, draped in black, older than memory, and the white-robed faithful moved around it in their patient circles. There a sultan and a shepherd dress alike. Our lord asked himself what kind of pilgrim he would be, for what a king does in Makka is talked of in every market of Islam for a generation. He chose to be a generous one. He gave gold freely in the streets of the holy city, that no pilgrim there should go hungry while the lord of Gharnāṭa was among them.

Then he waited a month for the days of the pilgrimage. On the eighth of Dhū al-Ḥijja he went out to Minā with the multitude. On the ninth he stood on the plain of ʿArafa from noon until the sun went down, among a hundred thousand voices crying *labbayka*, and asked God's forgiveness for himself and his people. The scribes of the dīwān later found that on that same day, far away in the West, the pestilence left the last town of the realm. Not one house in Gharnāṭa was still touched by it. The chronicler sets the two facts side by side and draws no conclusion. God knows best.

He came home on the twentieth of Ṣafar in the year 753, eight months after he had left. Word had outrun the caravan. Every court of Islam had heard of the sultan of al-Andalus who stood at ʿArafa and fed the poor of Makka, and they thought better of Gharnāṭa for it. The *ʿulamāʾ* were content as they had not been for years. Faraj handed back the seal of the regency. And from that day our lord carried a title that no victory could have given him: al-Ḥājj Yūsuf.

*Our Lord, give us in this world good and in the Hereafter good, and protect us from the punishment of the Fire.*
