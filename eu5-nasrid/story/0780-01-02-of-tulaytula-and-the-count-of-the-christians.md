The year 780 opened with the Emperor in [Ṭulayṭula](place:tulaytula). The city stands on its rock in a bend of the Tagus at the centre of Iberia. It had been the capital of the Visigoths, then a city of Islam for three hundred and seventy years, and the seat of a ṭāʾifa king of our own faith, before Alfonso of Qashtāla took it in the year 478. For three centuries since, Christians, Muslims and Jews had lived in it side by side, more or less, and scholars of all three had sat in its libraries putting the books of the Arabs into Latin. Now it was the Emperor's again. Twenty-nine thousand souls lived in it, nearly all of them Christian.

The court did not mean to hold it as a conquered city. On 2 Muḥarram 780 the Emperor declared that Ṭulayṭula would become the gem of Iberia, and every estate was set to the work: the ʿulamāʾ, the nobles, the merchants and the dhimmīs. The city's markets were rebuilt, and its prosperity rose by thirty in the hundred. Ṭulayṭula and the towns of its land, Wad el-Lub, Illīška, al-Yabānis, Mnṭalban and the rest, were declared lands of al-Andalus in full, no longer conquests held by the sword. A governor was set over the city to see it done.

The harder question was how to govern four hundred thousand Christians who had been Castilians a month before. The Emperor answered it from the history of [Qurṭuba](place:qurtuba). Under the Umayyads, the Christians of the capital had answered to the emir through one of their own: the *qūmis*, from the Latin *comes*, the Count of the Christians, who spoke for his community and was held to account for its taxes. A Christian judge beside him heard their cases by the old law of the Visigoths, and the Jews had a head of their own, the office Ḥasdai ibn Shaprūṭ had held under ʿAbd al-Raḥmān III. The treasury of the caliphs had found the arrangement cheap and sure. The Mālikī jurists had found it hard to bear that a Christian or a Jew should be set over revenue.

The Emperor revived the office as the first new branch of the bureaucracy he had promised the conquered lands. He was clear about its terms. As long as the crown paid for it, the counts would gather the dhimmīs' taxes more fully, their communities would be treated with more tolerance, and the ʿulamāʾ, though they would grumble, would have no real cause for complaint. Nor would the call to Islam slow in the new lands. If the crown ever stopped paying, the jurists' old objection would come back, the scholars' patience would wear thin, and conversion would falter. The Emperor meant to keep paying. The dhimmīs were content with the arrangement either way.

There was money for it, for once, because Qashtāla was paying reparations. Part of it went south. In the spring the Emperor spent five hundred dīnārs of Castilian gold to carry the famous silk of [Gharnāṭa](place:gharnata) into [Mursiyya](place:mursiyya), now in the crown's own hands: more mulberry groves, more silkworm houses, and more silk sent on to the markets of Europe, which had always paid well for it. The court remarked that Qashtāla, which had once taken tribute from the house of Naṣr, was now paying for its silk.
