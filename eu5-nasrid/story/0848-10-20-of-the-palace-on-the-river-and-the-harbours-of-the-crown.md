The anger of the Jamāʿa of 846 did not last. By the spring of 848 the ʿulamāʾ, the commoners and the dhimmī had settled back to the measure at which the crown liked to keep them. Even the umarāʾ grumbled less, and the burghers, who had been delighted with the world for a year, were merely satisfied with it. The peace of the realm was not what it had been, but it was mending, and the Emperor's right to rule had never stood higher in the eyes of his subjects. The princes, who had spent two years appeasing the estates, were able to go home.

The house grew in those years, and most of it grew around the admiral prince [Yūsuf](person:yusuf-ibn-ismail-ibn-muhammad). His wife [Muʾmina](person:mumina-bint-muslih-al-din) gave him his first son at [Gharnāṭa](place:gharnata) on 3 Jumādā al-Ākhira 846, and he was named [Abū al-Ḥasan ʿAlī](person:abu-al-hasan-ali-ibn-yusuf-ibn-ismail); he was third in line before he could walk. [Amie](person:amie-of-mali) of Mālī bore him two sons, [Saʿd](person:saad-ibn-yusuf-ibn-ismail) in 847 and [Fārūq](person:faruq-ibn-yusuf-ibn-ismail) in 848, both at Niani, at the court of her father Mansa [Tiramakhan](person:tiramakhan-mali). [Fāṭima](person:fatima-bint-yusuf-ibn-ismail) of Banbalūna gave him a daughter, [ʿĀʾisha](person:aisha-bint-yusuf-ibn-ismail-ibn-muhammad), at Gharnāṭa. The court joked that the prince now had heirs on both shores of the desert.

The house also lost one of its own. On 8 Jumādā al-Ūlā 847 the Emperor's brother [Saʿd](person:saad-ibn-ismail-ibn-saad) died at fifty-one. When the two of them were boys, the first Emperor had watched Saʿd closely, wondering whether he would make the better ruler. He had lived quietly since his brother took the throne, and he was buried quietly.

On 26 Jumādā al-Ākhira 848 the Emperor announced that he would build a new palace at [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya), on the river, where the sea-going ships came up to the capital. His grandfather had built mosques and his father madrasas. He meant to build a house from which an emperor of the sea could watch his ships come in, and he had it raised beside the quays.

Four months later he turned to the harbours themselves. On 20 Shawwāl 848 he ordered that the ports of the realm be taken out of the hands of the towns and the tax-farmers and placed under the crown's own officers. A governor of the sea would answer for them, and the harbour masters would answer to him. The order was to take nearly two years to carry through. The clerks of the dīwān al-inshāʾ said that the crown's hand would reach the coast a fifth more easily through its ports, and that the coastal towns would grow faster for it. The harbour masters, who had grown rich under the old arrangement, said nothing in public. The ports had a price, and the Emperor paid it in his own power. To make room for the new ordinance he let go the old one by which he had ruled alone, and gave up a measure of his say elsewhere in the realm so that his officers might hold every harbour. The princes thought it a fair exchange: the court could argue about the rest, but the sea would answer to the crown.

*And He subjected for you the ships to sail through the sea by His command, and subjected for you the rivers.*
