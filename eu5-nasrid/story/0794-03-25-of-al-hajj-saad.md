Five years after the season passed him by, the Emperor set out again, and this time he left in spring. He stayed at court only long enough for one more marriage. A princess of the house of ʿOthmān, the sultans rising in Anatolia, came west to marry his son: [ʿĀʾisha](person:aisha-ottoman), fifteen years old, who became the crown prince's second wife beside [Bernarda](person:bernarda-of-murcia). The wedding was hardly over before the caravan moved. On 6 Jumādā al-Ākhira 793 he left [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya) with half a year in hand, and the crown prince [Ismāʿīl](person:ismail-ibn-saad) took up the regency a second time.

This time the pilgrimage went by sea along the coast of the Maghrib. Off that coast a merchant ship hailed the Emperor's galleys, her decks stacked with bolts of silk and fine cloth from distant ports, and her captain would sell only after a merchant's unhurried haggling. The Emperor bought the finest cloth aboard for the caravan, and lost ten days to the bargaining. In Shaʿbān he came again to al-Qāhira, and the court that had watched him the first time watched him now. He was a creature of habit. He sat with the scholars of al-Azhar for another month, and took up with the sultan [Būlād](person:bolot-mamluk) where they had left off. But it was summer, not autumn, and when he rode out of the northern gate there were months to spare.

In the last days of Dhū al-Qaʿda 793 the Grand Mosque of Makka opened before him. The Kaʿba stood at its heart, black-draped, older than memory, with the white-robed faithful moving round it in patient circles, thousands of them from every land where the call to prayer is heard. Sultan and shepherd dressed alike. On the ninth of Dhū al-Ḥijja the Emperor stood with them on the plain of ʿArafāt beneath the Mount of Mercy, from noon until the sun went down, and the pilgrimage he had first meant to make in 774 was complete. He had been asked what kind of pilgrim he would be, since what a king gives in Makka is told in every market of Islam for a generation. He answered by distributing his gold freely, so that no pilgrim in the city went hungry that season.

He was in the holy city when the letter from Ishbīliya found him. [Aiman](person:aiman-mamluk) of the Baḥrī house, his first wife, sister of the Mamlūk sultan and mother of his son [Yūsuf](person:yusuf-ibn-saad), was dead at forty-seven. She had gone home to Miṣr to see her family while the caravan was on the road, and the letter said a beast of the Nile had taken her by the river. The Emperor read it twice and gave the order for prayers. Courts keep count of such things, and this one counted two great wives lost in three years: [Duʿāʾ](person:dua-marinid) while the Emperor was at court, and now Aiman while he was half the world away at Makka. It noted too that Aiman's son, twenty years old, now had no mother to speak for him, and that there were those in the crown prince's household who did not seem sorry. Nobody at court said any of this aloud.

On 25 Rabīʿ al-Awwal 794 the caravan came home. Its stores were lighter and its pilgrims were changed, and word had outpaced the camels. Crowds filled the road into Ishbīliya to welcome a ruler who had stood before the Kaʿba and come back to tell of it. From that day he bore the title al-Ḥājj, earned and not granted, as his father had. *Let it be known*, the heralds cried, *Makka and back, by the grace of Allāh.* The ʿulamāʾ were content with him as they had not been in years, and every Muslim court from [Fās](place:fas) to al-Qāhira sent its congratulations.

He met his grief as he always had, with work. Before the year was out he did a thing that would have been unthinkable twenty years earlier, when the ʿulamāʾ had come to him about the moneylenders of Ishbīliya. He gave moneylending a form and a law. Banking, the new craft of the Genoese and the Florentines, was admitted into the empire and set under rules, so that the houses of credit of Ishbīliya could stand beside those of Janawa. The ʿulamāʾ, freshly content with their Ḥājj, let it pass.

*And pilgrimage to the House is a duty owed to Allāh by all people who are able to find a way there.*
