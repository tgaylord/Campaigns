*Read, in the name of your Lord who created.* The first word God sent down to His Prophet was not *fight* or *rule* but *read*. In the years after the pestilence and the Sevillan revolt, our lord [Yūsuf](person:yusuf-i) set out to make it the first word of his government as well.

He began, as a prudent prince does, with the wealth that would pay for it. On the ninth of Jumādā al-Ākhira 757 he granted a charter to the weavers of [Gharnāṭa](place:gharnata). For a generation the silk of the city had been carried to every market from Genoa to Tūnis, and now the guilds of the city had a law of their own and the protection of the crown. The looms of the Albayzín, which the plague had half emptied, filled again.

In Shawwāl the roads followed. Engineers cut new ways through the mountains: south from Gharnāṭa to [al-Mariyya](place:al-mariyya) and its harbour, and north-east to the saffron fields of [Wādī Āsh](place:wadi-ash) and [Basṭa](place:basta). Tracks that a mule could barely climb in winter became roads that carried carts in every season. Every road the sultan built to carry silk would also carry teachers.

At the same time the libraries rose, in Gharnāṭa first and then in the other cities of the south. These were not the treasure-houses of a prince, locked away for his own pleasure. They were rooms where any man who could read might sit and read. The sultan's purpose was plain, and he did not hide it. More than a hundred years under Qashtāla had stripped [Qurṭuba](place:qurtuba) and the valley of the great river of their faith and their language. The mosques had been made churches, the schools closed, and the books burned or carried north. The Peace of Qurṭuba had given back the land. The libraries were meant to give back the people.

On the twelfth of Rajab 758 the second part of the reform began. Madrasas were founded in the northern cities as well, in the lands won from Qashtāla, where [Jayyān](place:jayyan) and [Ishbīliyya](place:ishbiliyya) had not heard the law taught for a century. The jurists who taught in them were paid from the treasury. They were told to teach the Book and the law to anyone who would listen, the sons of Christian farmers among them, and not to compel anyone. The ʿulamāʾ grumbled that teaching unbelievers was a strange use of the treasury. The sultan answered that the Prophet had freed captives at Badr for teaching ten Muslim children to read, and that he could at least pay for the same in reverse.

The court did not expect quick results, and it got none. In the year the northern madrasas opened, three in every ten souls in Qurṭuba were Muslims, the same as at the peace, and about one in ten in the valley around it. But a madrasa is not built for a year. It is built for a hundred.

*Read, and your Lord is the most Generous, who taught by the pen, taught man that which he knew not.*
