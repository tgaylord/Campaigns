The old Emperor had sent his kinsmen to sea. His son sent whoever would serve him best. In Jumādā al-Ūlā 811, while the dīwān was still busy with the lands of the north, four expeditions put to sea from the ports of [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya) and the coast. One went south, to follow the coast of Africa further than any ship of al-Andalus had gone. One went to the islands that had been found off the western coast, beyond Mali. And two went to the islands the Christian sailors called the Canaries and Madeira, which the geographers called the Eternal Isles. A fifth captain was at sea already: ʿAbd al-Ghafūr, an Andalusī of the merchant class with a rare gift for talking to strangers, had been in the sea of the Azores since before the year began.

The captain who went south was a Sephardi named Daniel, who had come to Islam and kept the name he was born with. The Sephardim had been under the Emperor's protection since the year of Avignon, and the scribes recorded with some pride that the first of them to command a ship of the empire had sailed further from home than anyone. By 813 he was in the gulf below the bulge of Africa, where the coast turns east and the stars of the north sink into the sea.

The captain who went to the islands beyond Mali was stranger still. He was García of Ponte de Sor, a Portuguese noble and a Christian, who in 808 had led the rebels of Estremadura against the walls of [al-Ushbūna](place:al-ushbuna) and been thrown back. Three years later he sailed under the Emperor's flag. The Emperor [Ismāʿīl](person:ismail-ibn-saad) had a way of turning men, and he never explained to the dīwān how he had turned this one. By 813 García was off the islands of the Green Cape, charting their harbours.

The voyages to the islands did more than chart them. On the first day of 1412 by the Christian reckoning, Funchal on Madeira became a town of the crown, the first the empire had ever held beyond the sea: eighteen hundred souls, peasants and slaves, who spoke Castilian and prayed as Muslims.

In Rabīʿ al-Awwal 815 news reached Ishbīliya that changed the worth of everything the captains were doing. A sea road to the Indies had been found. For as long as anyone remembered, the pepper and cinnamon of the East had come overland, through the hands of the Mamlūks and the Venetians, and every hand had taken its share. Now there was another way. The price of spices rose in every market of the West, and seven markets became the ports of the new trade: Genoa, Constantinople, Barcelona, Bordeaux, Naples, and two of the Emperor's own, Ishbīliya and al-Ushbūna. The merchants of the capital said the Emperor's father had moved the court to the river for exactly this day, and that he had only been thirty years early.

*Do you not see that the ships sail through the sea by the favour of Allāh, that He may show you of His signs?*
