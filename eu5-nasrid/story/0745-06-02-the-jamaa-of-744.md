In the spring of 744 the sultan called the Jamāʿa, the great assembly of the realm, where the nobles and the *ʿulamāʾ*, the burghers, the commoners and the dhimmīs each send their voices to the throne. He had meant to call it for war. Portugal was still divided against itself, and Qashtāla was weakened by it. The grievance against Qashtāla had lain sealed in the dīwān for two years. Some at court said the hour had come to use it.

But the hour passed before the Jamāʿa could meet. On the eighth of Dhū al-Qaʿda word came that Qashtāla and Arghūn had sworn an alliance, and that on the same day Qashtāla and Portugal had sealed an Iberian wedding pact. The three crowns of the Christians, which had fought one another for a hundred years, now stood together. In the middle of Dhū al-Ḥijja the tyrant [Alfonso](person:alfonso-xi) marched into Portugal to end its civil war in his wife's favour. The moment in the north was gone, and a realm still in debt could not fight all three crowns at once.

So when the Jamāʿa sat at the end of Dhū al-Ḥijja, our lord [Yūsuf](person:yusuf-i) turned it to other work. He would strengthen the crown that the Christians feared to see strong, and pay what the crown still owed. He asked the assembly to change the Distribution of Power itself, so that the balance of the realm's government should favour the ruler and not the estates. The debate was long and the nobles were bitter. But the sultan had paid down half their loans in four years, and he had won them a war. On the fifteenth of Muḥarram 745 the change was proclaimed. Where the old law had cut the crown's power by a tenth, the new law raised it by a third.

In those same days, while the delegates of the Jamāʿa filled the city, the sultan married. The lady [Sayyida](person:sayyida-of-fezzan), daughter of the house of Murzuq in Fazzān, had been betrothed to him at nine years old, in the first year of this chronicle. Now she was fifteen. The wedding was held in the Alhambra with the whole realm, in a manner of speaking, as its guests. The poets sang of the sultan who took a bride and a crown in the same season.

The marriage left its mark on the customs of the court. In Jumādā al-Ākhira the old aristocratic court, where the great houses had their places by right of birth, gave way to the royal harem, where the household of the sultan is its own world, and its women and their sons are bound to him alone. Some of the nobles said that the sultan had shut the door of the palace in their faces, and they were not wrong.

*And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them, and He placed between you affection and mercy.*
