The king of Ṣiqilliya did not get far. His army had been broken before Balarm, and what was left of it, two thousand archers and horsemen, fled west along the coast toward Trapani and the sea. The army of al-Andalus caught it at Sciacca on 23 Shawwāl 854. Not one of the Sicilians came off the field free. Al-Andalus lost a hundred and eight men.

The peace was made the same day. It was the peace al-Andalus wanted, because by then al-Andalus was the only party with an army in Sicily. The whole kingdom passed to the Emperor: Messina on its strait, Catania and Saraqūsa, Girgenti and Mazara, Trapani at the western point, and Balarm itself. Twenty-two towns in all. For the first time in nearly four centuries Balarm was governed in the name of a Muslim prince. It came with the palace of its Norman kings, its cathedral and its castle. The north of Sardinia went to Tūnis, as had been promised: Sassari, Alghero and five towns more, taken from Arborea. The Pope, who had done little in the war, was given a truce and nothing else. The king, of the old house of Barcelona, kept his life and his name.

Sicily had had subjects, and they had to be disposed of.

The first was Athens. It was a Christian duchy at the end of Greece, ruled by Catalans who had come east as soldiers a century and a half before and never gone home. It had stood against the Turks to its north and the Neapolitans to its west. It had been Sicily's vassal more in name than in fact, and now Sicily was gone. Duke Peter, a kinsman of the fallen king, would not stand alone. He would not swear to the Turks or to Naples either, his old enemies. So he sent to [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya) and made an offer: fealty, in exchange for being left to rule Athens as he always had. He was the same Peter who, as an admiral in the first winter of the war, had sailed a single ship against the prince [Yūsuf](person:yusuf-ibn-ismail-ibn-muhammad)'s squadron off Terracina and lost it. Yūsuf remembered the name and spoke for him. The Emperor accepted. Athens became the vassal of al-Andalus, with its own duke, its own church and its own laws.

The second was Negroponte, the long island off the coast of Attica. The king of Sicily had held its crown in his own person. By the terms of the peace that crown passed with Sicily to the Emperor, so that [Muḥammad](person:muhammad-ibn-ismail-ibn-saad) ibn Ismāʿīl, sultan and Emperor of al-Andalus, found himself lord of a Greek island with Venetian lords that he had never seen, with his son [Ismāʿīl](person:ismail-ibn-muhammad-ibn-ismail) as its heir. He was delighted, and ordered a map of it.

Not everyone across the water was pleased. On the day of the peace Naples, now the neighbour of al-Andalus across the strait of Messina, put its claim on record. The dīwān noted it and filed it.

Sicily was too large and too far from Ishbīliya to be governed from the palace by the river. The dīwān agreed that it should have a ṭāʾifa of its own, ruled by a house bound to the crown. But al-Andalus was still at war, in name, beside the Mamlūks in Arabia. [Aḥmad](person:ahmad-ibn-muhammad-ibn-ismail) counselled that the new ṭāʾifa be raised when that war was over and not before, and the Emperor agreed.

*God does not forbid you from those who do not fight you because of religion and do not expel you from your homes, from being righteous toward them and acting justly toward them. Indeed, God loves those who act justly.*
