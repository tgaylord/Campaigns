Arghūn had no army left, but [Barshalūna](place:barshaluna) still had its walls. They were the walls of the old counts of the city, built on Roman ones, with the sea at their back where the Catalan galleys could come and go. In the last days of Dhū al-Ḥijja 775, with the Feast of the Sacrifice kept in camp, our lord [Saʿd](person:saad-ibn-yusuf) drew his lines around the city. The siege that followed was not the work of a season. Summer passed, and autumn, and the sultan's engineers counted the days by the stones they had brought down. The townsmen counted them by what was left in their granaries.

The war was being fought on other shores too. The Mamlūk sultan [Būlād](person:bolot-mamluk) had promised to keep the second crown of King Joan busy, and he kept his word through the shaykh of Barqa, [Ramaḍān](person:ramadan-of-barqa). Ramaḍān was a Baḥrī of the sultans' own house, fifty-four years old, with four wives, eight children, and a soldier's temper. In the spring he had marched west along the coast of Ifrīqiya against the Jabal Nafūsa, the Berbers of the mountains who paid homage to Ṣiqilliya. He scattered their army at Zuwāra and finished it at Gharyān. Then he took ship. In Jumādā al-Ūlā 776 he landed two thousand nine hundred men on Māliṭa, the rock between Ṣiqilliya and Ifrīqiya, which the Duke of Athens held for the Sicilian crown, and took it. The Arabs had held that island once, and the Normans had taken it from them. The court at [Gharnāṭa](place:gharnata) said it was another front of the faith's return, from the Ebro to the sea of Ṣiqilliya.

The Sicilians did not let it go quietly. Three weeks later, on 6 Jumādā al-Ākhira, their fleet of thirty-one sail found Barqa's eight ships at anchor in the bay below the island, which the Christians call St George's. Not one of Ramaḍān's ships came out of it. Māliṭa stayed taken, but its garrison was cut off from home, and the Sicilians let the shaykh know that the sea around their island was still theirs.

In those same weeks the sultan settled a matter of his own house, and it was harder than the siege. His father had brought the finest tutors of the Islamic world to Gharnāṭa for him when he was a boy, and the court said the realm had been paying back the cost ever since in victories. Now there were two princes. The heir, [Ismāʿīl](person:ismail-ibn-saad), son of [Duʿāʾ](person:dua-marinid), was eight, and already had a tongue that could talk a qāḍī out of his own verdict. [Yūsuf](person:yusuf-ibn-saad), son of [Aiman](person:aiman-mamluk) the Mamlūk, was three and already following his father about the yard with a wooden sword. Duʿāʾ wrote from the regency that the tutors were an heir's right and an heir's alone. The sultan wrote back from the camp before Barshalūna that both were his sons. The masters were sent for, at great cost, and both boys sat before them. Duʿāʾ said nothing more about it, which the court took for a bad sign.

On 2 Shaʿbān 776 a second Mamlūk host was in Catalonia. There were four thousand two hundred and seventy-two of them, foot and horse, under another Baḥrī cousin of the sultans' house. They marched through [Jarunda](place:jarunda) for Empúries under the mountains, though the country had been stripped bare and they could draw no bread from it. Barshalūna was already under siege and hardly needed them. The court understood what they had come to say. The Marīnids of [Fās](place:fas), Duʿāʾ's house, had twice made war on Gharnāṭa in a dozen years. The Baḥrī sultans of Miṣr, Aiman's house, were fighting beside it in Ifrīqiya, at Māliṭa and now in Catalonia. Those who had spoken for the Mamlūk alliance were heard more often in the dīwān that winter, and Aiman's household had more visitors than it had had in years.
