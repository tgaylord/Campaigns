The Emperor's second son, [Yūsuf](person:yusuf-ibn-saad), had married twice: Madīna, of a house of the Mamlūk lands, and [Caterina](person:caterina-de-borja), daughter of King [Alfons](person:alfons-de-borja) of the Ṭāʾifa of Arghūn. The Christians of the north have a custom that a prince who marries an heiress goes to live at her father's court, and Yūsuf went. By the year 796 he was living at Benabarre in the Aragonese hills, a guest of the house of Borja, and in the spring of 800 he led its soldiers in the field. King Alfons had died in Rabīʿ al-Thānī 795, and his son [Rodrigo](person:rodrigo-de-borja), a boy of eleven then, had worn the little crown since. Rodrigo had no son, and Caterina was his heir.

So the old quarrel of the harem ended without anyone winning it. [Duʿāʾ](person:dua-marinid) and [Aiman](person:aiman-mamluk), who had fought for the better part of twenty years over whose son would inherit, were both in their graves. The crown prince [Ismāʿīl](person:ismail-ibn-saad) was heir beyond dispute, and his brother was across the mountains. But the question did not die. It went down a generation.

Ismāʿīl had three sons. The eldest, [Muḥammad](person:muhammad-ibn-ismail-ibn-saad), son of [Bernarda](person:bernarda-of-murcia) of Mursiyya, was twelve, stubborn and slow, and his tutors had given up making excuses for him. The second, [Saʿd](person:saad-ibn-ismail-ibn-saad), son of the Ottoman princess [ʿĀʾisha](person:aisha-ottoman), was six. He was quick, he loved his books, and he had been given the costliest teachers in the realm. The third, Aḥmad, was four and wanted everything his brothers had. By the law of the house, which the Emperor had inherited and never changed, the crown would pass from Ismāʿīl to Muḥammad.

Then there was Yūsuf's son, another [Saʿd](person:saad-ibn-yusuf-ibn-saad), born at Benabarre in Rabīʿ al-Thānī 796 to the heiress of Arghūn. He was four, and his teachers wrote that they had never seen a child like him. The old men of the court remembered another boy named Saʿd of whom the same had been said, fifty years before, and they looked at the Emperor when they said it. The Emperor did not hide that he had grown fond of the boy. He stood sixth in the line of succession.

The law could be changed. A sultan of the house could name whichever son he chose, as the caliphs had done. But a crown that changed its law for a favourite would shake the realm from end to end, and the realm had only just steadied itself after the giving of the ṭāʾifas. The umarāʾ would ask what other law the Emperor might change for his pleasure. The Emperor had spent thirty-five years telling the qāḍīs, the counts and the tax-farmers that the law bound everyone, and he would not now make himself the exception. He left the law as it stood. If Ismāʿīl's son Saʿd grew into a man fit to rule, the question would wait for Ismāʿīl's reign. If not, the Emperor would decide it himself, and pay the price.

What he did not leave was the boy. In Dhū al-Ḥijja 800 he sent to Benabarre and called his son Yūsuf home to [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya), and the little Saʿd with him, to be raised at his grandfather's court and under his grandfather's eye. Caterina stayed at her brother's court, with child by Yūsuf again. The crown prince welcomed his brother and his nephew with the fairest words in the dīwān, and everyone who heard them agreed that nobody could have said it better.

*Our Lord, grant us from among our wives and offspring comfort to our eyes, and make us a leader for the righteous.*
