After Jayyān the sultan did not go home. He turned the host down the valley of the great river, the Wādī al-Kabīr, toward [Qurṭuba](place:qurtuba). The farms and granaries of the Castilian frontier burned behind it, for the Christians had burned ours for a hundred years and had taught us how. On the eleventh of Rabīʿ al-Awwal, at [al-Quṣayr](place:al-qusayr) on the river above Qurṭuba, a Castilian army of seven thousand stood across the road. The sultan had eighteen thousand. The battle was short. Four thousand three hundred Christians fell, against fourteen hundred of ours, and the road to Qurṭuba lay open.

Our Moroccan brothers fared otherwise. On the far side of [Ishbīliyya](place:ishbiliyya), between that city and [Qādis](place:qadis), the Marīnid columns landed in small bands and were beaten in small bands. On the seventh of Rabīʿ al-Awwal a column of two and a half thousand was beaten at Sharīsh by ten thousand. On the third of Rabīʿ al-Thānī a column of nine hundred was destroyed before Ishbīliyya to the last man, without the loss of one Castilian. Castile's southern army, nearly ten thousand strong, grew fat on these easy victories. Its captains came to believe that every Muslim in the field would break as the Moroccans broke.

So the sultan swung west to relieve his allies. On the twenty-third of Jumādā al-Ūlā, at [Balma](place:balma) where the Shanīl runs into the great river, the Castilian southern army attacked him. He had sixteen thousand of his own and four thousand Moroccans who had gathered to his banner. The Castilians came on in the confidence of men who had won all summer and found they were not fighting Moroccans. By evening five thousand three hundred of them lay on the riverbank, more than half their army. Our dead numbered seven hundred. Of all the sultan's victories it was the cheapest.

In those same weeks word came from the East that Constantinople, the Queen of Cities, had fallen to the Ottomans, and that the Cross was taken down from its great church. The faithful of Gharnāṭa gave thanks in the mosques. The wiser heads at court reflected that the Turks had won their city alone, and that no one should count on help from the far end of the sea.

When the first year of the war was reckoned in Jumādā al-Ākhira, the account stood thus. The army of Gharnāṭa had won every battle it fought and lost fewer than six thousand men in them. The Moroccans had lost three and a half thousand in battle and six thousand more to hunger and sickness on the march. Castile had lost more than eighteen thousand, most of them to the sultan's sword. The dīwān held that Gharnāṭa had done almost twice Morocco's share of the war, and done it with half the men.

*If God should aid you, no one can overcome you.*
