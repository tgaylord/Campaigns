At the end of Jumādā al-Ākhira in the year 751 the pestilence was at last withdrawing. It touched only four towns of the realm now, and the physicians of the houses of healing began to send their assistants home. So the sultan ordered the dīwān to make a reckoning. It was to cover not only the realm as it now stood, but the old realm as it had been in the summer of 747, when the rumour first came out of the East, and every land beside it that had sent word.

The reckoning was this. The old lands of Gharnāṭa, which had held four hundred and ninety thousand souls when the merchants first spoke of Yāyiq, now held two hundred and eighty-four thousand. Two in every five had died. Of all the realms of which word came, only one far in the East had lost a greater share. Castile had lost a little more than a third of its people, and Aragon a third. Portugal had lost nearly as many. But Morocco had lost fewer than one in five, and Tūnis fewer than one in thirteen. The Mamlūks had lost fewer than one in six. Across the whole world some forty-six million had died.

Why had Gharnāṭa suffered worse than the Christians it had beaten? The dīwān found the answer in its own ledgers, and it was bitter. Gharnāṭa was a realm of cities as Castile was not. Two of every five of the sultan's subjects lived within the walls of [Gharnāṭa](place:gharnata), [Mālaqa](place:malaqa), [al-Mariyya](place:al-mariyya) and the other great towns. In Castile it was one in twenty. The Castilians live scattered in hamlets and walled farms, where a sickness can burn out in a single household. Our people live in the alleys of the Albayzín and the markets of the coast, where it ran from door to door. The cities of the realm lost almost half their people, the houses of healing notwithstanding. The few cities of Castile lost one in five.

And it took the people who had made the cities what they were. The burghers, the merchants of silk and sugar and the masters of the guilds, fell by three in five. The *ʿulamāʾ*, the scholars and the judges and the teachers of the madrasa, fell by more than half. The Nobility fell by nearly half, the Commoners of the fields by a little less. The soldiers, who had spent those years in camp on the frontier and not in the crowded streets, lost only one in seven. The old Gharnāṭa of the scholars and the silk merchants was gone. The realm that remained was smaller, more rural and more martial, and nearly half of it was Christian.

Yet the realm steadied. With the sickness passing, stability rose above twelve, higher than it had stood when the war began. Sovereignty climbed to fifty-eight parts in a hundred. The dead could not be recalled, but the living could still be ruled.

*In order that you not despair over what has eluded you and not exult over what He has given you. And God does not like everyone self-deluded and boastful.*
