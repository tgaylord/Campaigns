The hills of al-Andalus had fed the caliphs with silver and iron, and the Emperor [Saʿd](person:saad-ibn-yusuf) wanted to know what they still held. In Jumādā al-Ākhira 804 he sent a survey into the highlands: prospectors to walk the streambeds and hammer the outcrops, and assayers to test what they found. He gave the command to [Khālid ibn Yaḥyā Raḥḥū](person:khalid-ibn-yahya), fifty-nine years old, son of the captain of the Volunteers of the Faith, once of the dīwān and once the general who won the second half of the Marīnid war. Khālid had a builder's training and a soldier's legs, and he was the man the court sent whenever no prince of the house could go himself. When the treasurers asked how much the assayers might spend, the Emperor told them to spare nothing: a survey done cheaply would find only what it was paid to find.

The first find was a warning. At Almonaster in the Sierra the copper workings were set to roast their first heap of ore, and it gave off a yellow smoke that drove the men off the slope and withered the grass downwind. The farmers of the valley brought a complaint to the governor. The metallurgist offered to build kilns that would keep the smoke in, at a cost no one had counted. The court sided with the farmers, and the copper was left in the ground.

The hills paid for it afterwards. In Shaʿbān the prospectors found a seam of iron at al-Iskuriyāl in the Guadarrama, the rock streaked black and red and running deep into the mountain, where the headman's grandfather had spoken of old workings. In Ramaḍān they found silver at [Qusanṭīna](place:qusantina), the old silver town of the house of Naṣr: a vein wide enough to swing a pick in, so pure it could be polished straight from the rock. In Dhū al-Qaʿda, at Qrabaqa, they traced a scatter of bright gravel up a streambed to a pocket of crystal in the hillside, and the lapidaries called the stones exceptional. The Emperor ordered all three kept quiet. A merchant who tells the market what he has found, he said, sells it for less. Khālid's men came home to [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya) in Muḥarram 805, after seven months in the hills, and the finds had already repaid the assayers nearly three times over.

Silver needed coiners, and the Emperor gave the coinage to the burghers. They would strike good coin of honest weight from the new silver and gold, under the eye of the Ḥisba, and with it they were given grants to raise the cities. The burghers were delighted. The umarāʾ were not, nor were the ʿulamāʾ and the dhimmīs, and of all the estates the nobles were now the least content. The court sent them gifts to keep their temper. But the dīwān had already set its course. The state was built: its offices, its chancery, its inspectors and its banks no longer needed the great lords. From now on the crown would grow by taking from the umarāʾ, as gently as it could, and the burghers would rise in their place.

In the first days of Ṣafar 805 the Emperor called a Jamāʿa at Ishbīliya. The umarāʾ came for their gift, a monopoly on the land roads of trade for ten years. The burghers came to pay for their privileges, and they pledged a great sum to build up the markets of the south. On the day after the assembly rose, the Emperor granted Ishbīliya a charter for its jewellers, to double their trade with the silver of Qusanṭīna.

By Dhū al-Ḥijja of that year the workshops were rising. Ishbīliya had eight halls of jewellers and nine more half-built, and [Istijja](place:istijja) two and three more, and the great market of the capital was being enlarged again beside them. The treasury took in far more each month than it had on the eve of the War of the League, and spent a quarter less than it took. And still the umarāʾ, between them, sat on many times the gold the treasury held.

*And give full measure when you measure, and weigh with an even balance. That is the best way and best in result.*
