The crusade had been fought with ships and swords. The war that followed it was fought with ledgers, and it had been coming for years. The merchants of Ishbīliya had outgrown the western sea. Their offices ran from Niani to Cairo, the Emperor's arsenals had given them a fleet, and the markets of Ishbīliya, [Barshalūna](place:barshaluna) and al-Ushbūna were each the first of their region. The merchants of Genoa, who had been the masters of that sea before them, had watched every one of those offices open.

The first quarrel was at home. In the last days of Dhū al-Ḥijja 843 the Andalusī merchants of the crown brought a complaint to the dīwān. In the Ṭāʾifa of Arghūn, under King [Rodrigo de Borja](person:rodrigo-de-borja), the Catalan merchant houses still kept the privileges they had held since before the Restoration: their own consuls, their own courts, their own rates at the customs. Now that Barshalūna itself belonged to the crown, the merchants asked why the Catalans of Arghūn should trade on better terms than the Emperor's own subjects. The dīwān heard them and did nothing. The king of Arghūn was the Empress's brother, and the Emperor had a larger quarrel on his hands.

In the same weeks Genoa closed Italy to the merchants of al-Andalus. The Genoese did not do it alone. The court of King [Edward](person:edward-v-of-england) in London, which ruled France as well, had leaned on them all spring, and the embargo they proclaimed reached every port and market where Genoa's word counted. The answer from Ishbīliya came within days, in the first week of Muḥarram 844. No merchant of the Angevin crowns, English, French or Gascon, would be received in any port of the Emperor. The admiral prince Yūsuf was given the order to enforce it at sea.

The old merchants of the capital said that a trade war hurt both sides and helped neither, and that the Venetians would be the only ones to profit. The young ones said that the Franks would need Andalusī sugar, gold and paper long before al-Andalus needed English wool. The Emperor, who had read a good deal about trade by then, agreed with the young ones. He also ordered the treasurers to tell him every month what it was costing.

*Woe to those who give less than due, who, when they take a measure from people, take in full. But if they give by measure or by weight to them, they cause loss.*
