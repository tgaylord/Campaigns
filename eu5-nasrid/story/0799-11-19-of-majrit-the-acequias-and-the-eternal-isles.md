The amīr Muḥammad had built [Majrīṭ](place:majrit) as a watchtower, to guard the road to [Ṭulayṭula](place:tulaytula) from the Christians beyond the mountains. Five hundred years later the Emperor [Saʿd](person:saad-ibn-yusuf) looked at it with a merchant's eye. It stood in the middle of the peninsula, where the roads from the four quarters crossed, with the Guadarrama behind it for timber and iron and the plain before it for wheat. A town at a crossroads, he told the dīwān, will grow whether a king helps it or not, and a king who helps it will be paid back many times over. In Jumādā al-Ākhira 798 he set the crown to building a second heart for al-Andalus on the Tagus and the Manzanares. Ṭulayṭula, which had made blades since before the Arabs came, was given weaponsmiths. Mines and lumber mills went up toward the Guadarrama, and wheat farms on the plain, and seven building sites stood open between the two cities at once.

In Dhū al-Qaʿda 799 a new round of building began across the empire. Across the valley and the new lands of the north the crown threw fourteen bridges, and it raised madrasas at [al-Ushbūna](place:al-ushbuna) and [Shantarīn](place:shantarin), so that the children of the converts would learn their religion from Andalusī teachers. In the old lands [Mālaqa](place:malaqa) and [al-Mariyya](place:al-mariyya) were given madrasas too. Majrīṭ was given a guild of toolmakers and Ṭulayṭula a tannery. Along the great river, from the capital to [Qurṭuba](place:qurtuba), the crown dug a chain of acequias at [Qarmūna](place:qarmuna), [Istijja](place:istijja), [Shalūqa al-Kubrā](place:shaluqa-al-kubra) and Qurṭuba itself, canals that carried water from the hills to the fields and gardens. Another watered the fields of [Bīnār](place:binar). The Castilians had kept the old canals of the caliphs and called them by their Arabic name, acequia, from al-sāqiya. Now the Arabic word was spoken over them again. The Emperor rode out to see the first water come down the channel at Qarmūna, and asked the engineers what each cubit of it had cost before he praised them.

The same years carried the empire's ships further south than they had gone before. The geographers called the islands off the coast of the Maghrib al-Jazāʾir al-Khālidāt, the Eternal Isles, and placed the edge of the known world there. Two men of the Emperor's own family went to find them. [Muḥammad ibn Ismāʿīl al-Aḥmar](person:muhammad-ibn-ismail), the husband of his half-sister [Maryam](person:maryam-bint-yusuf), took ship from [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya) in Rabīʿ al-Thānī 798 at the age of sixty-two, an age at which most men of the house asked only for a garden. By the summer of 799 he was charting the Canaries and lay off Aguere on Tenerife, where the islanders still went about in goatskins and had never heard the call to prayer. His cousin [Ismāʿīl ibn Muḥammad al-ʿĀbid](person:ismail-ibn-muhammad-al-abid) sailed from Coria on the river for the seas beyond, toward the green cape far down the African coast, and in the summer of 799 he lay off Funchal on Madeira, gathering water for the long run south.

They were not alone on the Ocean. The ships of Qashtāla were in the sea of the Azores, and the sails of another kingdom had been seen off Madeira. The Christians of the north had begun to look at the Encircling Sea as the Emperor did.

*And We made from water every living thing. Then will they not believe?*
