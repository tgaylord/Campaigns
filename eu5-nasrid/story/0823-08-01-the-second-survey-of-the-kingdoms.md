Fifty years after the first survey of the kingdoms, the Emperor [Ismāʿīl](person:ismail-ibn-saad) found it in the treasury, still beside the map of the caliphs, with the leaf of three lines that his father [Saʿd](person:saad-ibn-yusuf) had kept. He read it and laughed at the last section, where the scribes had judged that Gharnāṭa was no longer far from the great powers. Then he ordered a new one. The Dīwān al-Inshāʾ had envoys at every court that mattered by now, and it did not need to question pilgrims and captives. Its survey was ready on the first day of Shaʿbān 823, the day after Fermoselle, and the Emperor read it on the road home.

**Of the three empires of the East.** The scribes began where the news was. Fifty years ago the East had been a quarrel of emirs and beys. Now it had three masters, and every envoy wrote of them in the same breath.

The greatest in land is [Tīmūr](person:temur), lord of Samarqand. He has reigned for sixty years and is eighty-four, and he has never stopped conquering. When the first survey was written his name was not on the list. Now his empire is the fourth power of the world, with seven hundred and seven towns, more than any realm but the empire of al-Ṣīn. Its frontiers run from Tabrīz to Kāshghar beyond the mountains, and in the last six years he has added Farghāna, Fārs, Kirmān and Hurmuz on the Gulf, where the spice ships of India put in. The envoys said three things of him: that he has never lost a battle, that he leaves pyramids of skulls outside the cities that resist him, and that his treasury is empty, because he spends everything on the next war. The dīwān has named him among the empire's rivals for years, which amused the envoys, since his nearest town lies a thousand leagues from Ishbīliya. He has named as his heir a captain called Köke, a conqueror and a reformer of armies, who is forty-eight. No one at Samarqand expects the empire to hold together long once Tīmūr dies, and no one there says so.

The second is the house of ʿUthmān at Constantinople, sixth in the world. Fifty years ago the scribes wrote that the Turks were twenty-seventh among the powers and nowhere near the Strait. They have two hundred and ninety-four towns now, from the Danube to Erzurum, and eighty-two ships, the largest fleet in the Middle Sea. Their sultan is [Aḥmad](person:ahmad-ottoman), son of the [Muḥammad](person:muhammad-ottoman) of the first survey, and brother of the Emperor's own wife [ʿĀʾisha](person:aisha-ottoman). He has reigned since 815. He is a pious man and nothing else; the envoys found him slow in council and slower in the field. But in Rabīʿ al-Awwal 820 his generals made a peace that brought Sivas and the lands of Eretna, Amasya and the Dobruja into the empire, and the Turks now hold all of Anatolia except Cilicia, which is the Mamlūks', and the hills of Karaman, which pay them homage. His son Murād is a better man than his father, and the Greeks and the Hungarians watch him.

The third is the realm of the Mamlūks, ninth in the world and the only one of the three that is a friend. The sultan [Aqṭāy](person:aktai-mamluk) is the son of [Būlād](person:bolot-mamluk), and so a cousin of the Emperor's late half-sister [Umm al-Fatḥ](person:umm-al-fath-bint-saad). He has reigned for twenty-four years, a soldier whom his men will follow anywhere and a man who has seen the world. He holds Cairo, Syria and Palestine, and through his vassals the Ḥijāz and the Holy Cities, nearly three hundred towns in all. His merchants' trade is nearly as rich as the empire's own. The alliance of 768 still stands, the oldest the empire has. The envoys warned that Aqṭāy names both the Turks and Tīmūr among his rivals, and that if either of them turned south the Mamlūks would need their friends.

**Of the Maghrib.** Fās has a new sultan. [Abū al-Faḍl](person:abu-al-fadl-marinid), the Emperor's ally in the War of Jillīqiya, died of a fever of the lungs in Muḥarram 820 after thirty-five years on the throne. His son had died hunting in 813, so the throne went to his grandson [Yaḥyā](person:yahya-marinid), who was fifteen and has ruled in his own name since his sixteenth birthday. He is a grandson of [Jadīla](person:jadila-bint-yusuf), so the blood of Yūsuf I sits on the throne of [Fās](place:fas). The envoys did not like him: a cold young man, they wrote, who is cruel to his servants. But Fās is fourteenth in the world, it has forty-four ships, and it keeps the alliance. So do the Ḥafṣids of Tūnis, who joined it a month after the Peace of Rajab. For the first time since the Almohads every Muslim crown of the West stands together, and al-Andalus stands at their head.

**Of the Franks.** England is the second power of the world, though half its strength lies in its vassals across the sea. Its king, William, is the only prince of Christendom who names al-Andalus among his enemies, and the only friend left to Qashtāla. France is fifth. Its king, Pierre, has the largest army in Christendom, more than forty thousand men, and is using it against the princes of the Low Countries. He names England among his enemies, and not al-Andalus. The crown of the Germans has passed to the duke of Saxony. The Pope, a man of fifty-nine named Guilhèm, sits at Rome again and not at Avignon. Hungary and Naples have one king, László, twenty-eight years old.

**Of the Peninsula.** [Pedro](person:pedro-of-castile) of Qashtāla still reigns at Oviedo, as he has for twenty-six years, over forty-five towns in the rain. The scribes counted Qashtāla sixty-sixth among the powers of the world, and they found that it kept no army at all, only four ships. Its heir, Fernando, is twenty-eight.

**Of al-Andalus.** Then the scribes set down the empire: third among the powers of the world, behind only al-Ṣīn and England. It rules a hundred and eighty-six towns of its own and as many again in its ṭāʾifas, from Porto to Barshalūna, and its trade is worth more than that of any realm in the West. The scribes noted one thing more, in red, as their predecessors had noted the enmity of France. Among the great powers of the world only England counts al-Andalus an enemy, and England has no army to speak of.

The Emperor read it all on the road from Fermoselle. He had a leaf copied, as his father had, and the clerks who saw it said it had four lines: Tīmūr is eighty-four, the Turks have the fleet, the Mamlūks are our friends, and Qashtāla has no army. He kept it with him.

*And if it were not for Allāh repelling some people by means of others, the earth would have been corrupted, but Allāh is full of bounty to the worlds.*
