The trade war that Genoa had begun in 844 did not end. It spread, one market at a time, and wherever it spread the court of the Angevin crowns in London had been there first.

On 8 Ṣafar 848 Ireland closed its ports to the merchants of al-Andalus. The Irish had no quarrel with Ishbīliya of their own. Their king was the Angevins' man, and he did as he was told. On 11 Shaʿbān of the same year the merchants of Aquitaine did the same. The wine of Bordeaux had been sold for a generation to Andalusī factors who paid for it in gold and sugar, and the Gascon merchants complained loudly that they were the ones being punished. Two years later, on 19 Shawwāl 850, Flanders followed. That was the heaviest blow. The cloth towns of Flanders were the greatest market of the north, and the Andalusī houses at Bruges had been buying and selling there since the days of the old Emperor.

The court at [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya) had kept its own embargo on the Angevins for six years, and it had not been enough. The day after Flanders closed, the Emperor [Muḥammad](person:muhammad-ibn-ismail-ibn-saad) ordered that the markets of al-Andalus be shut to every Angevin merchant: not only their ships kept out of the harbours, but their goods kept out of the funduqs, whoever carried them.

The young merchants of the capital applauded. The old ones counted. By Dhū al-Qaʿda 850 the treasury held a quarter of what it had held three years before, and the peace of the realm stood lower than at any time since the crusade. The Emperor still asked his treasurers every month what the war was costing. They told him, and he did not change course.

*And if you punish, punish with an equivalent of that with which you were harmed. But if you are patient, it is better for those who are patient.*
