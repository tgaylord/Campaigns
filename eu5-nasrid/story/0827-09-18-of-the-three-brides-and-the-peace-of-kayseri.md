The Emperor [Ismāʿīl](person:ismail-ibn-saad) had read his survey of the kingdoms and kept its leaf of four lines, and in the years after it the court watched him act on it through his grandsons. The elder, [Ismāʿīl](person:ismail-ibn-muhammad-ibn-ismail), the son of Magdalena, was married three times over. His first bride was Tella of the Marīnids, sister of the young sultan [Yaḥyā](person:yahya-marinid) of [Fās](place:fas). His second was Ayaulym, daughter of the Mamlūk sultan [Aqṭāy](person:aktai-mamluk). His third was Khadīja, daughter of the Ottoman sultan [Aḥmad](person:ahmad-ottoman), whose sister was already the Emperor's own wife. Of the three great Muslim crowns that the survey had counted around al-Andalus, the heir's heir was now the son-in-law of two and the brother-in-law of the third. His brother [Aḥmad](person:ahmad-ibn-muhammad-ibn-ismail), the abler of the two, was married nearer home: to Dīma of the Ḥafṣids of Tūnis, to Muʾmina, daughter of the lord of Burtuqāl, and to ʿĀʾisha bint Saʿd of the house. In 825 the first of the next generation were born. The secretaries of the Dīwān al-Inshāʾ said the Emperor had found a way to write letters that would still be read in fifty years.

The old generation went on dying. On 25 Rabīʿ al-Thānī 825 [Umm al-Fatḥ](person:umm-al-fath-bint-yusuf), the youngest child of Yūsuf I, died of dysentery at fifty-nine, and with her the last of the children of the sultan who had ruled Gharnāṭa as Qashtāla's tributary. In the north, on 27 Shawwāl 825, King [Pedro](person:pedro-of-castile) of Qashtāla fell down a flight of stairs in his castle at Santander and died of it. He was fifty-one, and he had reigned for twenty-eight years and lost almost everything. His son Fernando, a young man hungry for glory, inherited four towns and the Cantabrian coast. The preachers of Ishbīliya were too pleased to say anything unkind about the stairs.

The lord of Burtuqāl, the Emperor's cousin [Aḥmad](person:ahmad-ibn-ismail-ibn-yusuf), had a different kind of ambition. On 8 Dhū al-Qaʿda 824 he proclaimed his ṭāʾifa the Kingdom of Portugal and had himself crowned with the crown of its old kings, before the bishops of the country and in their church, since the kingdom's people were Christians and its laws were theirs. The Emperor allowed it. A king of Portugal who owed his crown to Ishbīliya was worth more to him than a lord of Burtuqāl who might one day forget it. The ʿulamāʾ were uneasy until they learned that the new king had grown more zealous in his own faith with every year on the throne, and that he prayed in his own mosque at Barcelos while his subjects went to their cathedrals.

At court the dīwān's plan of 822 was carried out. Once [Balansiyya](place:balansiyya) was integrated, the Catalans took their place beside the Andalusīs and the Portuguese as subjects whose tongue was no bar to any office in the empire, and the Sephardim, who had been under the Emperor's protection since the year of Avignon, were accepted with them. The Castilians, as before, were not.

Then the East called. On 13 Shawwāl 826 Aqṭāy of Miṣr declared war on the Ottomans, before, he said, they could declare it on him, and he called his allies in after him. The letter reached Ishbīliya on 25 Shawwāl. The court did not like it. The Mamlūks were the oldest friends al-Andalus had, but the Ottoman sultan was the Emperor's brother-in-law and the father of one of his grandson's wives, and the Mamlūk sultan was the father of another. The Emperor answered the call on the day it came, and all seven ṭāʾifas answered with him, because an alliance of fifty-six years was worth more than one war. But the court let it be known in Cairo, as politely as the Dīwān al-Inshāʾ knew how, that al-Andalus was a long way from Anatolia, and hoped that Aqṭāy did not expect much of it.

He did not need much. The Mamlūk armies, seventy thousand strong, went up through Cilicia into the plateau, and the Ottomans, for all their sixty thousand men and a hundred and seventy ships, could not stop them. The mountain people of Nafusa, who had joined the Ottomans, made their peace on 25 Shaʿbān 827. On 12 Ramaḍān the Ottomans made theirs. Thirty-seven towns of central Anatolia passed to the Mamlūks, from Kayseri, Niğde and Nevşehir to Malatya, Harput and Çorum. The armies of al-Andalus fought no battle the court thought worth recording, and the empire took nothing and lost nothing. The Emperor sent his congratulations to Cairo and his condolences to Constantinople by the same ship, and the court said it was the shortest and cheapest war the house of Naṣr had ever fought.

*And if two factions among the believers should fight, then make settlement between the two.*
