The peace of 779 had been made for fifteen years, and the Christians spent the last of them afraid. In Jumādā al-Ākhira 795 Qashtāla, Portugal and the little mountain lordship of Andorra swore a league against al-Andalus. The court at [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya) took it as a compliment, and when the war came it named the war after the League. Its spies had been inside Qashtāla for two years. By the turn of the Christian year the dīwān had its claim ready, and the ʿulamāʾ gave the cause its name. The chroniclers of Qashtāla had long written of their wars on al-Andalus as a recovery of what the Goths had lost. The ʿulamāʾ turned the word around: al-Istirdād, the Recovery, of what the Christians had taken. They counted the War of the Upper March and the War of the Aragonese Restoration as its first two wars. This would be the third. Five days later, King Fernando of Qashtāla died at fifty-nine, and his son [Pedro](person:pedro-of-castile), twenty-three, stubborn and fond of his wine, inherited the war his father had seen coming.

On 1 Jumādā al-Ūlā 796 the Emperor [Saʿd](person:saad-ibn-yusuf) declared it. The four ṭāʾifas marched with him, and so did the Marīnids of [Fās](place:fas) with their vassals and the Mamlūks of Miṣr with theirs. With Qashtāla stood Portugal, Andorra, and the Pope in Rome, with his cities of Siena and Faenza.

The sea war went both ways. In Shaʿbān the fleet of Fās, seventy-two sail, caught the Pope's squadron at the mouths of the Rhône and sank all fourteen of its galleys. Far out on the Ocean, though, a Castilian squadron of only eleven ships hunted the Catalan fleet down three times: off Galicia, at the mouth of the Duero, and in the bay of Setúbal. Of the thirty-three ships Catalonia had sent out, three came home. The Castilians lost none.

On land, the Christians struck first and struck the weakest. In Shawwāl Qashtāla's great host, twenty-four thousand men, came down past Sigüenza onto the small armies of the ṭāʾifas. Arghūn's fell back in time. [Balansiyya](place:balansiyya)'s did not, and it died there to the last man, three thousand nine hundred and fifty-eight of them, for a hundred and forty Castilians. In the same week the Portuguese came down into the Alentejo and destroyed both armies of the Ṭāʾifa of the Algarve, at Setúbal and at Montemor.

The Emperor had seventeen thousand six hundred men and no wish to meet twenty-four thousand on their own terms. He followed them north instead. Through Shawwāl and Dhū al-Qaʿda his host shadowed the Castilians up the Aragonese border, from [Qalʿat Ayyūb](place:qalat-ayyub) toward the Ebro and Tudela, with the Catalans and Aragonese on their other flank, waiting for the Castilians to make a mistake. On 16 Dhū al-Ḥijja they made it. They had chased an Aragonese army of nearly nine thousand into the hills of Navarre, and at [Banbalūna](place:banbaluna) they found the Emperor across their road. Their pursuit was cut off and their host beaten, nearly two and a half thousand of theirs lost for fewer than fifteen hundred of ours, and the Castilians went back the way they had come in haste.

They came south again with the cold weather, still nearly twenty-two thousand strong, under the best general left to Qashtāla. [Agustín Arce](person:agustin-arce) was Grand Master of the Order of Alcántara, fifty years old, a soldier since boyhood. On 10 Ṣafar 797 the Emperor caught him on the plain of the Cinco Villas at Ejea de los Caballeros. It was ground of Arce's choosing, and the officers told the Emperor so. He attacked anyway, and he did not command it from a hill. He was forty-six, and for nine days he rode among his men from first light, ate what they ate and slept where they slept. Through all nine the Grand Master held, and the knights of Alcántara were all that the chronicles of their order would later claim. On the ninth day they broke. Ten thousand eight hundred Castilians were dead or scattered, against some four thousand seven hundred of ours. That one field moved the war further than the whole of the summer before it.

What was left of Arce's host fled west into Old Castile, nearly eleven thousand men with no fight left in them. On 12 Rabīʿ al-Thānī, at Peñafiel on the Duero, fewer than nine hundred horsemen of the Ṭāʾifa of Arghūn found them. They rode for the boy-king [Rodrigo](person:rodrigo-de-borja), whose father had died two years before, and they cut down or scattered more than ten and a half thousand men without losing one of their own. Qashtāla had no army left in the field.

Only in Tuscany did the war go badly for the faithful. There the men of Barqa and a Mamlūk detachment, sent to keep the Pope busy at home, were beaten by the Sienese, twice at Grosseto, once at Piombino and, when the spring came, at Montalcino. The Pope stayed busy, though, and he sent nothing more to Qashtāla.

*Indeed, Allāh loves those who fight in His cause in a row as though they are a single structure joined firmly.*
