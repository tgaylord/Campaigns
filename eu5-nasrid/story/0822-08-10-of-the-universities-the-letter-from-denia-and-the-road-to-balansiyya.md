The madrasas of 815 taught the children of the towns their letters. Three years later the Emperor [Ismāʿīl](person:ismail-ibn-saad) wanted something above them. On 22 Rabīʿ al-Awwal 819 the first universities of al-Andalus opened their doors, five of them at once: at [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya), where the court sat; at [Qurṭuba](place:qurtuba), the old capital of the caliphs; at [Gharnāṭa](place:gharnata), where the house of Naṣr began; at [Ṭulayṭula](place:tulaytula), the city of the translators; and at [Mālaqa](place:malaqa), the port. The Emperor's charters gave each of them the same instruction. They were to teach law and grammar, as the mosques always had, but also medicine, astronomy, the measuring of the earth and the reckoning of accounts, and their doors were open to any subject of the empire who could pay or find a patron to pay for him. The umarāʾ asked what the sons of merchants would do with astronomy. The Emperor said they would find their way to the Indies with it, and that the umarāʾ's sons were welcome to come and learn it too.

The new scholars soon found an old quarrel waiting for them. In Rabīʿ al-Awwal 822 a letter more than three hundred years old began to pass from hand to hand among the secretaries of the dīwān. Its author was Ibn Gharsiya, a Basque Christian who had been carried off as a child and raised a Muslim at the court of [Dāniya](place:daniya), in the age of the first ṭāʾifas. He had written it against the old Arab families who sneered at men like him. The Arabs, he wrote, were masters at describing camels. Philosophy, astronomy and the measuring of the earth had belonged to the peoples they conquered long before the Arabs came, and those peoples still knew them better. In his own day the letter had caused a scandal. Now the secretaries read it aloud to each other and laughed, because half of them were the grandsons of converts themselves. The Berbers had carried the conquest of al-Andalus, the Slavs had commanded the guard of the caliphs, and the sons of converted natives had filled the chanceries, and still the old houses counted a man's descent before they counted his learning.

The Emperor heard of it and asked for the letter. When he had read it he gave the dīwān his answer in four words that the secretaries copied into the margins of their own copies: a man is what he knows. He sent invitations to scholars of every nation to come to the new universities, and the old Arab families had to accept Berbers, converts and the sons of Christians in office beside them. The court said the Emperor had put it more shortly than Ibn Gharsiya, and had not needed to insult anyone's camels to do it.

The answer had a purpose nearer home. The integration of [Balansiyya](place:balansiyya), a ṭāʾifa of the crown for eighty years, was nearly complete. Its people spoke Catalan and had nearly all come to Islam, and the dīwān meant them to stand at court as the Portuguese of al-Gharb and Burtuqāl already stood, as subjects whose tongue was no bar to office. The Castilians were not invited. They were the most numerous of the Emperor's subjects, and their king still sat at Oviedo, and the court left them out, when it noticed them at all.

On 10 Shaʿbān 822 the Emperor ordered a great road built from Ishbīliya to Balansiyya, over the mountains of the old kingdom of Murcia. When Balansiyya came into the crown a governor would sit there, and the road would carry the empire's orders to him, and his influence out into the Middle Sea, as fast as a rider could go. The dīwān noted a second purpose that no one put in the charter. In the crusade of 806 the Emperor's father [Saʿd](person:saad-ibn-yusuf) had marched over the Pyrenees to Avignon. A road that carried a governor's letters to the coast would carry an army to the French border just as fast, should a Pope ever preach another.

*And of His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth and the diversity of your languages and your colours. Indeed in that are signs for those of knowledge.*
