In Muḥarram 851 news came from the east by way of the Mamlūk post. The empire of the Tīmūrids, which in the Emperor's youth had frightened every court from Cairo to Constantinople, had gone thirteen years without Temür and found no one to fill his place. Okhotur sat on Temür's throne. The provinces did as they pleased, and the great host did nothing. The merchants of [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya) who traded with Tabrīz said the roads were worse every year. The court heard it as one hears of weather in another country.

Nearer home there was a different change. For a century the Christian princes had written of al-Andalus as of a thing finished, a chapter closed long ago. Now their envoys came to Ishbīliya. They came from Italy, from the princes of the Empire, from the Angevins' rivals and even, quietly, from the Angevins. They brought gifts and careful compliments, and each of them asked, in his own way, what the Emperor meant to do.

The Emperor [Muḥammad](person:muhammad-ibn-ismail-ibn-saad) received them on 18 Jumādā al-Ūlā 851 in the new palace by the river. He had built it for exactly this, and he enjoyed every hour of it. His secretaries remembered that he had read the history of ʿAbd al-Raḥmān II the night before, and the embassy of the Greek emperor Theophilos to [Qurṭuba](place:qurtuba) in the year 225. When the last envoy had finished, the Emperor gave them one answer for all their masters: "We have arrived." The court wrote it down as a boast. The heir [Ismāʿīl](person:ismail-ibn-muhammad-ibn-ismail) said it was a promise, and a harder one to keep. Al-Andalus held no mastery in Europe, only a seat regained at a table its fathers had once headed. It would keep the gifts of the Restoration as long as it kept its weight among the powers of Europe, and the dīwān set itself to make sure it did.

The answer had a second half, and the jurists gave it its name. If al-Andalus was the heir of Qurṭuba, it was the heir of the Umayyads. And the Umayyads had not ruled al-Andalus alone. In their first age, from Damascus, their writ had run from the Pyrenees to the Oxus: across the whole Maghrib, through Egypt and Syria and Arabia, and into Persia. The jurists drew up the claim, and the Emperor had the map of it drawn and hung in the hall of the dīwān.

Then the dīwān looked at the map. Iberia was already the Emperor's, or his ṭāʾifas'. Fās and all the Maghrib al-Aqṣā belonged to the Banū Marīn, the Emperor's oldest allies, whose blood ran in his own house through his grandmother [Duʿāʾ](person:dua-marinid). Ifrīqiya belonged to the Banū Ḥafṣ of Tūnis, whose war in Sicily al-Andalus had joined in Rabīʿ al-Awwal. Egypt, Syria and the Ḥijāz belonged to the Mamlūks of Miṣr, the realm's friends for eighty years. Ismāʿīl had married princesses of Fās and of Egypt. Only Persia, at the far edge of the map, belonged to anyone else, and that was the empire that was falling in on itself.

Ismāʿīl laughed when he saw it. A claim on the lands of one's friends, he said, was a sword that does not leave the scabbard, and a sword in the scabbard is still a sword. The old Mamlūk party at court did not laugh. Its men had served the alliance with Cairo all their lives, and they asked the Emperor to say plainly that the claim was a scholar's exercise. Prince [Aḥmad](person:ahmad-ibn-muhammad-ibn-ismail) studied the roads on the map and said nothing about the borders.

The Emperor gave no order either way. The map stayed on the wall.

*And fulfil the covenant of God when you have taken it, and do not break oaths after their confirmation while you have made God, over you, a witness. Indeed, God knows what you do.*
