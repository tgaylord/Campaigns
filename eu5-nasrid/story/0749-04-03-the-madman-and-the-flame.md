On the first of Rabīʿ al-Awwal in the year 749 the host destroyed one more Castilian column before Qurṭuba, two thousand men caught in the open. It was a small victory and the last of that spring, for the pestilence was already in the land behind the army. Two days later God tested the realm in its head.

The sultan had come back from the frontier and was at [al-Manṣūra](place:al-mansura) in the east of the realm. As he came out from the prayer a man rushed through the crowd with a blade and fell on him. He was a madman, the guards said. Others said, more quietly, that he had been paid. Our lord [Yūsuf](person:yusuf-i) took the blade across the face, and the guards cut the man down before he could strike again. The sultan lived. He bears the scar to this day, and the people say it has made him look more like the warrior he has shown himself to be.

But the court asked why the guards had been so far from his side at the mosque door. Some whispered that they had been drawn away on purpose, by men who opposed the sultan's rule, his strengthened crown and his costly war. No one was named and no one was punished, which only made the whispers louder. The blow fell on the realm's sovereignty as much as on the sultan's face. The war, the pestilence and the knife together drove it down to two parts in five.

In those weeks the scales of the Strait tipped in a new direction. For the first time, the sway of the Marīnids over Gharnāṭa outweighed the sway of Castile. On the fourteenth of Rabīʿ al-Awwal the great fleet of Morocco, fifty-four ships, fell on the Aragonese off Cape Ben Sakka and sank thirteen of their sixteen galleys. It was the first victory the allies had won at sea in the whole war, and it made the Marīnid sultan bold and his envoys at Gharnāṭa overbearing. The court had gone to war to keep Castile's hand off the realm. It began to fear that it was only exchanging one master for another.

So the sultan looked further east. He sent a delegation to Kostantîniyye to congratulate the Ottoman beys on their conquest and to ask, after ten years of friendly letters, for soldiers against the crowns of Iberia. Süleyman Bey received them courteously and sent them home with this answer: *Why should we try to rekindle a nearly extinguished flame?* By this he meant al-Andalus.

The war was now costing fifty dīnārs a month against an income of nineteen. Five loans were owed again, and the treasury held eight hundred and nineteen. The realm was winning every battle and paying for every one of them.

*And it is not for a soul to die except by permission of God at a decree determined.*
