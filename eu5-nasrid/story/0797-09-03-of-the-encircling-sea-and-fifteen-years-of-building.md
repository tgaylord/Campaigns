The geographer al-Idrīsī, who wrote for a Norman king in Ṣiqilliya, tells of eight cousins of [al-Ushbūna](place:al-ushbuna) who fitted out a ship with water and food for months and sailed west into the Ocean, to learn what was in it and where it ended. The geographers called that Ocean al-Baḥr al-Muḥīṭ, the Encircling Sea, because it was believed to run around the whole inhabited world. The sailors called it the Sea of Darkness. The cousins found islands, some of them deserted and some of them inhabited, and a people who held them prisoner until a king sent them home blindfolded. The Ushbūnīs called them the Mugharrirūn, the men who were led astray, and named a street of the city after them. Two hundred and fifty years after the Christians took al-Ushbūna, it is a city of al-Andalus again, and the empire has its own Mugharrirūn at sea. This book tells how al-Andalus turned to the Ocean.

The court dates it from the Jamāʿa of 7 Muḥarram 781. That assembly moved the capital from the mountains to [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya), for its river and its market. A capital on a river that runs down to the Ocean needed ships. The empire had four of them. It also had the Catalan shipwrights, who had already been sent to build galleys for the Mamlūks in five ports, and now they built for their own sovereign. By the declaration of 796 the fleet numbered sixteen. Its first admiral of the blood was the crown prince [Ismāʿīl](person:ismail-ibn-saad). He took his first command at seventeen, on 15 Rabīʿ al-Thānī 785, and held it for three and a half years, through his father's pilgrimage and his own regency, until the Ḥijāba called him into the dīwān.

Every dīnār the crown did not spend on the madrasas and on settling the new lands went into trade. The hills of the Sierra Morena, around [Qusanṭīna](place:qusantina), [Qarmūna](place:qarmuna) and [Istija](place:istijja), were set to cutting timber for the shipwrights, and the empire's lumber mills went from nine to twenty-two. In the marshes of [Walba](place:walba), [Labla](place:labla) and [Ayamunt](place:ayamunt), and on the bay of [Qādis](place:qadis), the crown raised eight smelters for the bog iron that lies under the reeds, and eight charcoal-burners in the woods to feed them. More iron came out of the hills of the north. The silver of Qusanṭīna, which the house of Naṣr had held since the bargain of 752, went to the jewellers, and their guilds went from seven to fifteen, most of them at Ishbīliya. There were four new workshops for tools at [Gharnāṭa](place:gharnata) and [Ṭulayṭula](place:tulaytula) and five for cloth. In the fields of [Sharīsh](place:sharish), [Baṭalyaws](place:batalyaws) and [Mursiyya](place:mursiyya) cotton was planted again. Under the caliphs it had been grown all along the great river, until the Castilians let it die.

The trade needed markets, and the crown built nine more, with three great warehouses at Ishbīliya. It needed honest weights, and the Ḥisba gave it inspectors to check them. It needed money that could travel without a camel to carry it, and the banking offices went from one, a Genoese house at Ishbīliya, to eight, in [Qurṭuba](place:qurtuba), Gharnāṭa, [Mālaqa](place:malaqa), Ṭulayṭula and the capital. The trade of the African coast brought men as well as gold, as it always had, and two slave markets opened at Ishbīliya. And because a city of al-Andalus is not a city without them, the crown built eleven hammams, from al-Mariyya to Baṭalyaws.

The scribes counted it all on the eve of the Peace of Ramaḍān. In the hundred and eighteen towns the crown held through those fifteen years, its buildings had grown by a hundred levels, from five hundred and ninety-eight to six hundred and ninety-eight. The crown's income, a hundred and one dīnārs a month when the court came down to Ishbīliya, was a hundred and forty-five on the eve of the war. By then [Khayr al-Dīn al-Jayyānī](person:khayr-al-din-al-jayyani) was charting the coast toward Mali, the Emperor's ships had gone out to the sea of the Azores and to Madeira, and the crown was fitting out new voyages to the Canaries and the islands beyond them.

*And it is He who subjected the sea, that you may eat from it tender meat and bring forth from it ornaments to wear. And you see the ships ploughing through it, that you may seek of His bounty.*
