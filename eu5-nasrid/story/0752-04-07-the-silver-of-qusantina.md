The dīwān of Gharnāṭa watched the last year of the war as a merchant watches two men bidding against each other for the same bale. Castile was bled white by the pestilence and stood at the very edge of defeat. A little gold sent to the Marīnids might have pushed it over: gold for ships and for the wages of the raiders on the coast of Aragon. Morocco was bled less, but it was beaten on this shore and ruled now by the young sultan Abū ʿInān Fāris. And Gharnāṭa, which had made its own peace, held the balance between them.

So in Dhū al-Qaʿda of the year 751 the sultan's envoys went quietly to the court of [Alfonso](person:alfonso-xi). They made no threat, for a threat can be answered. They spoke instead of what the sultan might do and had not yet done. Gharnāṭa, they said, had no wish to see Castile humbled further. It would send no silver across the Strait, so long as the border between the two realms were made a little more just: a mild shift along the river below [Ishbīliyya](place:ishbiliyya). They asked for [Qarmūna](place:qarmuna) on its hill, and north of it, in the dark hills of the Sierra Morena, [Qusanṭīna](place:qusantina).

They called the shift mild, and the Castilians may have believed it. Qarmūna is a small town of potters. But the hills of Qusanṭīna are full of silver, and the dīwān had reckoned that their mines could yield the crown more than all of Ishbīliyya. On the twenty-fourth of Rabīʿ al-Awwal 752 the king of Castile, with the Marīnids at his throat and no silver to spare for pride, set his seal to it.

Thirteen days later, on the seventh of Rabīʿ al-Thānī, the Marīnids surrendered to Castile. They kept the islands they had taken from Aragon. And among the terms the Castilians demanded was one the court had not looked for: Morocco was to annul its treaties with Gharnāṭa. The alliance that had dragged the realm into the war, which the sultan had kept only to see what Fās would do, was broken for him by the hand of his enemy. Castile had done Gharnāṭa's work for it, and perhaps believed it had wounded Gharnāṭa.

When the war of 747 began, the realm held nineteen towns of the Strait's three lands, and its sovereignty was slipping below the half. At its end the realm held fifty-five of the seventy-two. Sovereignty stood at sixty-one parts in a hundred, stability was near fourteen, and the treasury owed nothing to anyone. Its only ally now was the lord of Egypt, whose daughter would marry its heir. It was still Castile's tributary in name. But the balance had moved, and it had moved toward Gharnāṭa. The last seventeen towns of the Strait still flew the banners of Castile, from Qādis on the coast to Runda and Marballa within the realm's own mountains.

*And they planned, and God planned. And God is the best of planners.*
