In Ṣafar of the year 759 the masons came to [Qurṭuba](place:qurtuba) again, this time to build for God and not for the governor. Beside the great mosque of the caliphs, which the Castilians had made a cathedral and which still stood as one, our lord [Yūsuf](person:yusuf-i) began a Great Madrasa. It was meant to be the largest college west of Fās. Qurṭuba's Muslims were few, perhaps three in ten, but there were more each year: converts from the Christian quarters, Andalusīs back from exile in the mountains, students drawn by the libraries. The old mosques of the city could no longer hold them, and the old learning of the city had no house. The Great Madrasa was to be both.

It was not the only work that year. By Ramaḍān the dīwān's register of works read like a census of the realm's ambitions. There was a madrasa rising in [Jayyān](place:jayyan) and a guild of spinners in [Gharnāṭa](place:gharnata). There were marketplaces in [Istijja](place:istijja), [Ishbīliyya](place:ishbiliyya) and [Mālaqa](place:malaqa), and a road of gravel to Yuz Aryuba. The dīwān paid for its new clerks out of the crown's own coffers, so that no estate could claim to own them.

The mind behind much of this sat in the dīwān and not on the throne. [Duʿāʾ](person:dua-marinid), the Marīnid wife who had taken a seat in the council in 756, had become the most talked-about minister in the realm. She had grown up in the palaces of Fās and had seen how the Marīnids ran their colleges and their chanceries. She brought those methods to Gharnāṭa: the endowments that paid for the madrasas, the registers that counted their students, and the habit of asking what a thing cost before praising it. Scholars and merchants praised her for the fresh air she brought into a court that had grown stale in the lean years. The nobles were troubled. A foreigner, a woman and a Marīnid sat at the sultan's right hand, and they did not like any of the three. They said so to each other, and once, unwisely, to the sultan. He told them that the Prophet's wife Khadīja had been a merchant and his wife ʿĀʾisha a scholar, and asked which of them they would have advised him against.

The Great Madrasa took years to finish, as such houses do. But from the year it was begun the call to prayer was heard more often in Qurṭuba. By the year the War of Independence began, nearly four in ten of the city's people were Muslims.

*God raises those who have believed among you and those who were given knowledge, by degrees.*
