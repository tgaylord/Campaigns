While the sultan walked toward Makka, the regent [Faraj](person:faraj-ibn-ismail) did not sit idle in the Alhambra. He governed as his brother would have wished, which is to say he built.

On the ninth of Shaʿbān in the year 752 he founded the Great Madrasa of [Gharnāṭa](place:gharnata) beside the great mosque. For years the scholars of the city had begged for a college where the law might be taught under one roof, as it is in Fās and al-Qāhira. The pestilence had taken more than half of them, and those who remained pressed the harder for it, lest the learning of al-Andalus die with them. The regent paid two hundred and eighteen dīnārs, and the walls began to rise. It was meant to draw students and jurists from across the Strait and to bind the learning of the cities to the service of the amir.

Five days later God showed how much such a house was needed. [Najīb al-ʿĀbidīn](person:najib) of the *ʿulamāʾ*, the most gifted mind left at court, was claimed by Qashtāla at its demand as overlord. He went north without a struggle. There, before the cathedral chapter of the Christians, he was baptised and took the name Ramiro. He carried with him in his saddlebags the letters, treatises and reckoning tables that were the inheritance of the libraries of Qurṭuba. The Christian scholars rejoiced over what he brought them. This was the second great mind of Gharnāṭa to go over to the Cross, after Ibn al-Khaṭīb. The court began to say bitterly that Qashtāla no longer needed to take our towns when it could take our men.

In Ramaḍān the regent turned to the silver of [Qusanṭīna](place:qusantina), which was the realm's own at last. He opened the treasuries to the merchants. Gold and silver were to flow out freely to the markets of the world, where they would buy more than they could ever buy as coin in the mint at home. And on the thirteenth of Dhū al-Qaʿda, in the days when the sultan came down into Makka, Faraj raised within the great tower of the palace a hall to receive the envoys of kings. Its cedar ceiling was carved with the seven heavens, thousands of pieces set above the throne, so that every embassy might see the order of creation under which the house of Naṣr sits. It cost a hundred dīnārs, a fifth of a year's income. It speaks for Gharnāṭa more plainly than any letter.

So when al-Ḥājj Yūsuf came home in Ṣafar 753, he found his city richer in learning and in splendour, though poorer by one great scholar. And he found himself looking north. Gharnāṭa had been the capital of a mountain kingdom of eighteen towns. The realm was now forty-three towns, its heart lay in the valley of the great river, and its greatest cities were [Qurṭuba](place:qurtuba) and Ishbīliyya. Qurṭuba had been the seat of the caliphs when al-Andalus was the light of the West. The sultan began to speak of moving the throne back to it. The court was divided, and the old families of Gharnāṭa were alarmed.

But one matter outweighed all the others, and it was not in Gharnāṭa. In the new lands, in the villages around [Jayyān](place:jayyan) and [Ishbīliyya](place:ishbiliyya), the Castilian peasants were murmuring. They had buried a third of their people in the plague years, and they paid their taxes to a Muslim lord. They had not been asked whether they wished to change masters. The Castilian Patriots who had gathered in the hills since the peace were no longer a band of malcontents. They were becoming a cause. A throne in Qurṭuba would sit in the middle of it.

So the crown offered a concession. For a time the *jizya*, the tax that the dhimmīs pay for the protection of the Muslim state, would not be collected. The Christians of the new lands would keep in their own purses what they had paid to Gharnāṭa every year since the peace. The *ʿulamāʾ* grumbled that the law of God was not the sultan's to suspend, and that a tax forgiven to rebels is a reward for rebellion. The sultan heard them and did it anyway. But he told those closest to him that he doubted it would be enough. A man who hates his master, he said, does not love him more for being charged less.

*And hold firmly to the rope of God all together, and do not become divided.*
