When the Jamāʿa met in Muḥarram 781, the question before it was the one the conquests had been asking for ten years. The house of Naṣr had ruled from [Gharnāṭa](place:gharnata) for a hundred and forty years. The city had been chosen because the mountains defended it, and the mountains had kept the Christians out of it through every war since. But the frontier was no longer in the mountains. It ran now past [Ṭulayṭula](place:tulaytula) and [Baṭalyaws](place:batalyaws), many days' ride north, and the lands won from Qashtāla were still only half held. The court found itself far from the provinces it most needed to govern. A governor's letter took a week to reach the dīwān, and the answer took another week to go back.

The elders of the court spoke first, for [Qurṭuba](place:qurtuba), seat of the caliphs, at the heart of al-Andalus, with the Great Mosque of ʿAbd al-Raḥmān still standing in it. It was the capital a restored al-Andalus seemed to call for, and for a time the assembly leaned toward it. Then a second proposal was put. The empire should invest in the sea. The Catalan shipwrights of [Barshalūna](place:barshaluna), who had built galleys for the Mamlūks at al-Iskandariyya, were the Emperor's own subjects now, and they could build the empire a merchant fleet. If the empire was going to live by trade, its capital belonged where the trade was. [Ishbīliya](place:ishbiliyya) was the greatest city of the realm, with seventy-eight thousand souls and the first market in the world, and its river ran down to the Ocean. The assembly agreed, and so did the Emperor, who had always read a ledger as closely as a map: he chose the river and the market over the prestige of Qurṭuba. The court would move to Ishbīliya before the year was out.

The move would not be cheap. A capital needs roads, and the Emperor ordered them in every direction: south to [Sharīsh](place:sharish), east to [Mālaqa](place:malaqa), and north across the Guadiana to Baṭalyaws and to Ṭulayṭula itself. Each road would take as long as its length and its ground required. To pay for them the crown borrowed from the estates, five loans of a hundred and thirteen dīnārs each. The nobles and the merchants lent willingly enough, since the roads would carry their goods as well as the Emperor's letters.

In Shaʿbān 781 the court came down from the hills. The dīwān sat in the palaces of Ishbīliya, and the Emperor's grip on the city, which had never been more than half certain, tightened within months. By the end of the year seven stretches of new road were finished, and the rest were under way.

Gharnāṭa was not abandoned. The Emperor decreed that the city of his fathers would keep the honours of a capital. Al-Ḥamrāʾ still stood on its hill, and the court that sat there kept its full weight: the fortress its strength, the crown its authority, and the realm the learning and the dignity that had been gathering in those halls for a century and a half. Gharnāṭa had become the realm's ancestral seat, and in a realm that called itself a restoration, that was worth a great deal.

*It is He who made the earth tame for you, so walk among its slopes and eat of His provision.*
