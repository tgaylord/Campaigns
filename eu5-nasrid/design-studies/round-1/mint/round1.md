# Designer C, round one: the chronicle as a made thing

## 1. Dār al-Sikka: the Mint Cabinet

**Question:** what was the realm *worth* at each moment, and how did it change from one hand to the next?

**What the reader sees and does.** A wooden coin cabinet. Every save strikes one gold dinar, so there are 72 coins in drawers, one drawer per reign (Yūsuf I, Saʿd, Ismāʿīl, Muḥammad). Naṣrid coins carried writing rather than faces, so each coin is generated from its words and numbers. The field holds the ruler's name and title, which turns from *sulṭān* to *imperator* in 1375. The rim carries the Hijri date and the mint city, which moves from Gharnāṭa to Ishbīliya in 1379. The coin's diameter tracks the realm's locations (18 rising to 196). Its weight shows the treasury, and the fineness of the gold shows debt. A war won adds a countermark, a plague year leaves the flan clipped. Laid out in a row, the drawer becomes a chart you can feel: you see the coins shrink in 1346 and 1363, and grow after 1375. Tap a coin to flip it. The reverse is that save's ledger: estates, subjects, the dīwān, the Strait. Provenance shows up as relief. Figures read from the save are struck sharp, estimated estate powers are worn faint, and the writer's colour is a later countermark. On a phone it's a tray you swipe through. The printable version is a sheet of coin rubbings.

**Data.** Uses checkpoints.json and realm-readings.json, plus people.json for names and titles. Nothing is missing. One checkpoint has no economy recorded, so that coin is shown unstruck.

**Carries over.** Only the coin grammar is specific to this campaign: aniconic and legend-only. The pattern of one minted token per save, with its encoding rules, works for any campaign. A Christian campaign gets portrait coins, a Ming one gets cash coins with a square hole.

**Effort:** medium. **Risk:** too many encodings make the coin unreadable, so it needs a legend card and an ordinary table one tap away.

## 2. The Naṣrid Deck (the Mamlūk suits)

**Question:** who and what made this history, and how do they measure against each other?

**What the reader sees and does.** Playing cards reached Iberia from the Mamlūks, and their four suits became the Spanish ones, so the deck suits the campaign. **Swords** are the wars and battles: 194 battles, with hosts, losses and war-swing printed on the face. **Coins** are treasury, trade offices and loans. **Cups** are the court: marriages, births, estates and reforms. **Polo-sticks** are the reach: places, islands, expeditions. The court cards follow the Mamlūk order of *malik*, *nāʾib* and *thānī nāʾib*, which here means ruler, heir and vizier or ṭāʾifa king, and they carry the stats and traits. Every card has a front in chronicle names and a back in plain names, and a corner glyph saying whether it came from a save or from Trent's account. The reader browses by suit, or plays two small games. In *Chronology* you place a dealt card into a growing timeline, which teaches the order of 105 years. In *Trumps* you set two battles, sultans or wars against each other on a stat. Each new save ships as an expansion pack: "new cards since 1442".

**Data.** Uses events, people, places, wars and battle-places. Card art has to be generated, as geometric patterns per type, because the data has no images.

**Carries over.** The card templates, the expansion-per-save model and both games work for any campaign. Only the suit system is cultural: Latin suits for a Christian realm, money suits for China.

**Effort:** medium to large, mostly in writing a short line of text for each of hundreds of cards. **Risk:** events are uneven, and 131 player notes don't map to good cards. That calls for curation rules, such as only certain types become cards.
